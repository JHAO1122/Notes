    Chapter 11: M-Estimators and Z-Estimators (Part I)

    In this chapter, we study the consistency and asymptotic normality of M-estimators (introduced by Peter J. Huber) and Z-estimators.

    Maximum likelihood estimators (MLEs) and moment estimators (ME) can be viewed as special cases of M-estimators and Z-estimators, respectively.

    • Suppose the parameter (or “functional”) \(\theta\) of interest is attached to the distribution of the observations.

    • The sequence of observations follows the distribution:

    \[ X_n := (X_1, \dots, X_n) \sim p_\theta(X) \]

    1. Basic Definitions and Examples

    1.1 M-Estimators

    Definition 12.1 (M-estimator)

    The goal of an M-estimator is to find an estimator \(\hat{\theta}_n := \hat{\theta}_n(X_1, \dots, X_n)\) that maximizes some type of random (empirical) criterion function:

    \[ \theta \mapsto M_n(\theta) \]

    For example, the usual form is the sample average:

    \[ M_n(\theta) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n m_\theta(X_i) \]

    1.2 Z-Estimators

    Often, the maximization problem can be solved by setting the derivative (or gradient) equal to zero. This naturally leads to the concept of Z-estimators.

    Definition 12.2 (Z-estimator)

    A Z-estimator satisfies the following estimating equations:

    \[ \Psi_n(\theta) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \psi_\theta(X_i) = 0 \]

    More generally, a Z-estimator is an estimator \(\hat{\theta}_n\) that is a root of the above estimating equation.

    Some remarks on M- and Z-estimators:

    • M- and Z-estimators do not require the observations to be independent and identically distributed (IID) or to have an independent structure.

    • The minimization problem of the objective function \(-M_n(\theta)\) may be non-convex.

    • In practice, Z-estimators are usually solved by numerical optimization algorithms, such as (quasi-)Newton methods, gradient descent, stochastic gradient descent, etc. (especially when facing non-convex problems).

    1.3 Classical Examples

    Example 1: Maximum Likelihood Estimation and Pseudo-MLE

    • MLE: Let \(X_1, \dots, X_n \sim p_\theta\). The maximum likelihood estimator maximizes the likelihood function \(\prod_{i=1}^n p_\theta(X_i)\), which is equivalent to maximizing the log-likelihood function:
    \[ M_n(\theta) := \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \log p_\theta(X_i) \]
    • Pseudo-MLE: In this case, the \(X_i\) may be dependent, but we still borrow and maximize the above log-likelihood criterion function.

    Example 2: Location Estimators

    The sample mean and sample median can both be viewed as Z-estimators; they respectively satisfy the following estimating equations:

    • Sample mean:
    \[ \Psi_n(\theta) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \theta) = 0 \]
    • Sample median:
    \[ \Psi_n(\theta) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \text{sign}(X_i - \theta) = 0 \]

    Example 3: Quantiles

    For a distribution function \(F\), the quantile function is defined as:

    \[ F^{-1}(p) := \inf\{x \in \mathbb{R} : F(x) \ge p\} \]

    • Median: \(\text{med}(X) = F^{-1}(0.5)\)

    • General quantile: The parameter can be obtained by minimizing an expected loss, i.e., \(\theta_0 = \arg\min_\theta E \rho_\tau(X - \theta) = F^{-1}(\tau)\).

    Introduce the check function as the loss function:

    \[ \rho_\tau(y) = y(\tau - I_{(y<0)}) \]

    Thus, the \(\tau\)-sample quantile \(\hat{\theta}\) can be viewed both as an M-estimator and as a Z-estimator:

    • As an M-estimator criterion function:
    \[ M_n(\theta) := \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \rho_\tau(X_i - \theta) \]
    • As a Z-estimator estimating equation:
    \[ \Psi_n(\theta) := \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \left( (1-\tau) 1\{X_i < \theta\} - \tau 1\{X_i > \theta\} \right) = 0 \]

    For small sample size \(n\), van der Vaart's book gives an alternative definition of the quantile \(\hat{\theta}\) by solving the inequality: \(-1 < n\Psi_n(\theta) < 1\).

    Example 4: Huber Estimator

    The Huber estimator originates from robust statistics; it aims to control the influence of extreme outliers on the estimation result. It is based on the Huber \(\psi\) function:

    \[ \psi(x) = [x]_{-k}^k := \begin{cases} -k & \text{if } x \le -k \\ x & \text{if } |x| \le k \\ k & \text{if } x \ge k \end{cases} \]

    The Huber estimator solves the following estimating equation:

    \[ \Psi_n(\theta) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \psi(X_i - \theta) = 0 \]

    When \(k\) is large, the behavior of the Huber estimator is closer to the non-robust sample mean; when \(k\) is small, its behavior is closer to the highly robust sample median. Therefore, it achieves an excellent balance between the mean and the median.

    (Note: The empirical Z-estimator equation image often appears step-like (e.g., the empirical equation for quantiles), while its population expectation equation is a smooth curve.)

    2. Consistency of M-Estimators

    For an estimator \(\hat{\theta}_n\), we wish to prove that as the sample size tends to infinity, it converges in probability to the true parameter: \(\hat{\theta}_n \xrightarrow{p} \theta_0\), where \(\theta, \theta_0 \in \Theta\), and \(\Theta\) is equipped with a distance metric \(d\).

    Assume \(\hat{\theta}_n\) maximizes the random (empirical) criterion function:

    \[ \hat{\theta}_n = \arg\max_{\theta \in \Theta} M_n(\theta) = \arg\min_{\theta \in \Theta} -M_n(\theta) \]

    where \(-M_n(\theta) =: L(P_n, P)\) can be viewed as the empirical loss function.

    Definition 12.3 (True parameter)

    The true parameter \(\theta_0\) is usually defined as the maximizer of the deterministic (true) criterion function \(M(\theta) =: E m_\theta(X)\):

    \[ \theta_0 = \arg\max_{\theta \in \Theta} E m_\theta(X) = \arg\min_{\theta \in \Theta} E [-m_\theta(X)] \]

    By the Law of Large Numbers (LLN), for any given \(\theta\), we have \(M_n(\theta) \xrightarrow{p} M(\theta)\). However, it is important to note that this pointwise convergence is usually not uniform over the entire parameter space \(\Theta\)!

    To prove consistency, given an arbitrary random function \(\theta \mapsto M_n(\theta)\), we consider a sequence of estimators \(\{\hat{\theta}_n\}\) that satisfies the following nearly maximization condition:

    \[ M_n(\hat{\theta}_n) \ge \sup_{\theta \in \Theta} M_n(\theta) - o_P(1) \ge M_n(\theta_0) - o_P(1) \]

    (For example, this condition holds when \(-M_n(\theta)\) is strongly convex, i.e., if and only if the second derivative \(\ddot{M}_n(\theta) \ge O(1)I_p > 0, \forall \theta \in \Theta\).)

    2.1 Basic Theorem for Consistency of M-Estimators

    Theorem 12.1 (Consistency of M-Estimators)

    Let \(M_n\) be a sequence of random functions and \(M\) a fixed deterministic function. If for every \(\epsilon > 0\) the following three conditions hold:

    • C1. Uniform convergence:
    \[ \sup_{\theta \in \Theta} |M_n(\theta) - M(\theta)| \xrightarrow{p} 0 \]
    • C2. Well-separation / Identifiability:
    \[ \sup_{\theta: d(\theta, \theta_0) \ge \epsilon} M(\theta) < M(\theta_0) \]
    • C3. Nearly maximization: The sequence \(\{\hat{\theta}_n\}\) satisfies the aforementioned nearly maximization condition.

    Then we must have \(\hat{\theta}_n \xrightarrow{p} \theta_0\).

    Proof of Theorem 12.1 (click to expand)

    By the well-separation assumption C2, for any \(\epsilon > 0\) there exists \(\eta > 0\) such that:

    \[ M(\theta) < M(\theta_0) - \eta \quad \text{for all } \theta \text{ with } d(\theta, \theta_0) \ge \epsilon. \]

    Replacing \(\theta\) by \(\hat{\theta}_n\), we obtain the set inclusion: \(\{d(\hat{\theta}_n, \theta_0) \ge \epsilon\} \subseteq \{M(\hat{\theta}_n) < M(\theta_0) - \eta\}\).

    Therefore, in probability:

    \[ P\{d(\hat{\theta}_n, \theta_0) \ge \epsilon\} \le P\{M(\theta_0) - M(\hat{\theta}_n) > \eta\} \quad \text{--- (12.2)} \]

    Next, we show that the right-hand side of (12.2) tends to 0. Combine conditions C3 and C1. From C3 (nearly maximization):

    \[ M_n(\hat{\theta}_n) \ge M_n(\theta_0) - o_P(1) = M(\theta_0) - o_P(1) \quad \text{--- (12.3)} \]

    Note that the last equality in (12.3) holds because C1 implies pointwise convergence in probability: \(M_n(\theta_0) \xrightarrow{p} M(\theta_0)\).

    Further using (12.3) and C1:

    \[ M(\theta_0) - M(\hat{\theta}_n) \le M_n(\hat{\theta}_n) - M(\hat{\theta}_n) + o_P(1) \le \sup_{\theta \in \Theta} |M_n(\theta) - M(\theta)| + o_P(1) \]

    By the uniform convergence C1, the supremum term above is \(o_p(1)\). Hence, overall \(M(\theta_0) - M(\hat{\theta}_n) = o_P(1)\).

    Letting \(n \rightarrow \infty\) and substituting into (12.2), this implies the limiting probability is 0, and thus:

    \[ d(\hat{\theta}_n, \theta_0) \xrightarrow{p} 0 \]

    The proof is complete. \(\square\)

    (Note: If the objective function \(M(\theta)\) is very flat near the maximum, or if there exist local maxima arbitrarily close to the global maximum, then the well-separation condition C2 will not hold.)

    2.2 A Corollary under Modified Conditions

    Corollary 12.2 (Consistency of M-Estimators under Modified Conditions)

    Assume the following conditions hold:

    • (i) The uniform convergence condition C1 of Theorem 12.1;

    • (ii) Unique maximization: \(M(\theta) := E m_\theta(X)\) attains a unique maximum at \(\theta_0\);

    • (iii) Compactness: The parameter space \(\Theta\) is compact;

    • (iv) Continuity: The deterministic function \(M(\theta)\) is continuous.

    Then, for any estimator \(\hat{\theta}_n\) satisfying the nearly maximization condition \((v) \ M_n(\hat{\theta}_n) \ge M_n(\theta_0) - o_P(1)\), we have \(\hat{\theta}_n \xrightarrow{p} \theta_0\).

    Proof of Corollary 12.2 (click to expand)

    For any given \(\delta > 0\), define the exterior set of the neighborhood \(B_\delta^c(\theta_0) := \{\theta : d(\theta, \theta_0) \ge \delta\}\). By conditions (ii)-(iv) (a continuous function attains its maximum on a compact set), there exists a point \(\theta^* \in \Theta \cap B_\delta^c(\theta_0)\) such that:

    \[ \sup_{\theta \in \Theta \cap B_\delta^c(\theta_0)} M(\theta) = M(\theta^*) < M(\theta_0) \]

    Next we show that for any \(\epsilon > 0\), when \(n\) is sufficiently large, the following chain of inequalities holds with probability approaching 1 (wpal):

    \[ M(\hat{\theta}_n) > M_n(\hat{\theta}_n) - \epsilon/3 > M_n(\theta_0) - 2\epsilon/3 > M(\theta_0) - \epsilon \quad \text{--- (12.4)} \]

    We analyze each inequality in (12.4) one by one:

    First ">" (based on C1): Define the event \(E_1 := \{M(\hat{\theta}_n) \le M_n(\hat{\theta}_n) - \epsilon/3\}\). Since \(\sup_{\theta \in \Theta} |M_n(\theta) - M(\theta)| \xrightarrow{p} 0\), we must have \(P(E_1) = o(1)\).

    Second ">" (based on condition v): Define the event \(E_2 := \{M_n(\hat{\theta}_n) \le M_n(\theta_0) - \epsilon/3\}\). Because nearly maximization requires \(M_n(\hat{\theta}_n) \ge M_n(\theta_0) - o_P(1)\), we have \(P(E_2) = o(1)\).

    Third ">" (based on C1): Similar to the first term, define \(E_3 := \{M_n(\theta_0) - 2\epsilon/3 \le M(\theta_0) - \epsilon\} = \{M_n(\theta_0) \le M(\theta_0) - \epsilon/3\}\). Again by pointwise convergence, \(P(E_3) = o(1)\).

    Now set \(\epsilon = M(\theta_0) - M(\theta^*) > 0\). Substituting this \(\epsilon\) into the head and tail of the inequality chain yields that with probability approaching 1:

    \[ M(\hat{\theta}_n) > M(\theta^*) \quad \text{wpal} \]

    More precisely:

    \[ P(E_1^c \cap E_2^c \cap E_3^c) \ge 1 - \sum_{i=1}^3 P(E_i) \rightarrow 1 \]

    Note that because \(\theta^*\) is the maximizer on the boundary region \(\Theta \cap B_\delta^c(\theta_0)\), if \(M(\hat{\theta}_n) > M(\theta^*)\) then it must be that \(\hat{\theta}_n\) lies inside the central neighborhood:

    \[ \{M(\hat{\theta}_n) > M(\theta^*)\} \subseteq \{d(\hat{\theta}_n, \theta_0) < \delta\} \]

    Hence, letting \(n \rightarrow \infty\):

    \[ 1 \leftarrow P\{M(\hat{\theta}_n) > M(\theta^*)\} \le P\{d(\hat{\theta}_n, \theta_0) < \delta\} \le 1 \]

    This proves \(d(\hat{\theta}_n, \theta_0) \xrightarrow{p} 0\). \(\square\)

    3. Consistency of Z-Estimators

    Since Z-estimators are solved by setting the objective function equal to zero (rather than maximizing), their consistency theorem is a natural generalization of that for M-estimators.

    Theorem 12.3 (Consistency of Z-Estimators)

    Let \(\Psi_n\) be a sequence of random vector-valued functions and \(\Psi\) a fixed vector-valued function. If for every \(\epsilon > 0\) the following conditions hold:

    • C1*. Uniform convergence:
    \[ \sup_{\theta \in \Theta} \|\Psi_n(\theta) - \Psi(\theta)\| \xrightarrow{p} 0 \]
    • C2*. Well-separation (Identifiability):
    \[ \inf_{\theta: d(\theta, \theta_0) \ge \epsilon} \|\Psi(\theta)\| > 0 = \|\Psi(\theta_0)\| \]
    • C3*. Nearly zero condition: The sequence of estimators \(\{\hat{\theta}_n\}\) satisfies \(\Psi_n(\hat{\theta}_n) = o_P(1)\).

    Then we must have \(\hat{\theta}_n \xrightarrow{p} \theta_0\).

    Proof sketch: This theorem follows directly from the consistency theorem for M-estimators. Simply construct the criterion functions \(M_n(\theta) = -\|\Psi_n(\theta)\|\) and \(M(\theta) = -\|\Psi(\theta)\|\). It can be seen that the nearly zero condition in Z-estimators corresponds exactly to the nearly maximization condition in M-estimators.

    3.1 Weakening the Uniform Convergence Condition

    In practice, the uniform convergence \(C1^*\) required in Theorem 12.3 is often too stringent and difficult to verify (it usually requires empirical process theory to establish rigorous guarantees). Lemma 12.4 provides a way to prove consistency without requiring uniform convergence.

    Lemma 12.4 (Consistency via Monotonicity/Continuity, vdv p47)

    Let \(\Theta\) be a subset of the real line, \(\Psi_n\) a random function, \(\Psi\) a fixed function, and for each given \(\theta\), we have pointwise convergence: \(\Psi_n(\theta) \xrightarrow{p} \Psi(\theta)\).

    Assume one of the following conditions holds:

    • (a1) Continuous and unique root: Each mapping \(\theta \mapsto \Psi_n(\theta)\) is continuous and has exactly one zero \(\hat{\theta}_n\); or
    • (a2) Monotone and bounded: The mapping is non-decreasing (or non-increasing), and \(\Psi_n(\hat{\theta}_n) = o_P(1)\);

    And assume:

    • (b) Sign alternation of the true root: Let \(\theta_0\) satisfy that for every \(\epsilon > 0\), \(\Psi(\theta_0 - \epsilon) < 0 < \Psi(\theta_0 + \epsilon)\).

    Then \(\hat{\theta}_n \xrightarrow{p} \theta_0\).

    Proof of Lemma 12.4 (click to expand)

    Case 1: Continuous and unique root (when a1 holds)

    If the mapping \(\theta \mapsto \Psi_n(\theta)\) is continuous and has a unique zero at \(\hat{\theta}_n\), then the event \(\{\Psi_n(\theta_0 - \epsilon) < 0 \text{ and } \Psi_n(\theta_0 + \epsilon) > 0\}\) must imply that the zero lies between them, i.e., \(\{\theta_0 - \epsilon < \hat{\theta}_n < \theta_0 + \epsilon\}\).

    In probability terms, this is expressed as:

    \[ P(\Psi_n(\theta_0 - \epsilon) < 0, \Psi_n(\theta_0 + \epsilon) > 0) \le P(\theta_0 - \epsilon < \hat{\theta}_n < \theta_0 + \epsilon) \]

    Since for the given \(\theta_0 \pm \epsilon\) we have pointwise convergence in probability \(\Psi_n(\theta_0 \pm \epsilon) \xrightarrow{p} \Psi(\theta_0 \pm \epsilon)\), and by condition (b), the probability on the left tends to 1 as \(n \rightarrow \infty\). Therefore, the probability on the right must also tend to 1, which proves the consistency of \(\hat{\theta}_n\).

    Case 2: Non-decreasing and nearly zero (when a2 holds)

    Suppose the mapping \(\theta \mapsto \Psi_n(\theta)\) is non-decreasing. First note:

    \[ \{\Psi_n(\theta_0 - \epsilon) < -\eta\} = \{\Psi_n(\theta_0 - \epsilon) < -\eta, \hat{\theta}_n \le \theta_0 - \epsilon\} \cup \{\Psi_n(\theta_0 - \epsilon) < -\eta, \hat{\theta}_n \ge \theta_0 - \epsilon\} \]

    Due to monotonicity of \(\Psi_n\), if \(\hat{\theta}_n \ge \theta_0 - \epsilon\), then \(\Psi_n(\hat{\theta}_n) \ge \Psi_n(\theta_0 - \epsilon) < -\eta\). Let \(E_- := \{\Psi_n(\hat{\theta}_n) < -\eta\}\). By the condition \(\Psi_n(\hat{\theta}_n) = o_P(1)\), we have \(P(E_-) = o(1)\) for any \(\eta > 0\). Therefore, the above union can be bounded as:

    \[ \subseteq E_- \cup \{\hat{\theta}_n \ge \theta_0 - \epsilon\} \]

    Similarly, we can define the event for the right tail \(E_+ := \{\Psi_n(\hat{\theta}_n) > \eta\}\), and also \(P(E_+) = o(1)\).

    Hence, for any given \(\epsilon, \eta > 0\):

    \[ P(\Psi_n(\theta_0 - \epsilon) < -\eta, \Psi_n(\theta_0 + \epsilon) > \eta) \le P(\theta_0 - \epsilon < \hat{\theta}_n < \theta_0 + \epsilon) + P(E_-) + P(E_+) \]

    Rearranging:

    \[ P(\Psi_n(\theta_0 - \epsilon) < -\eta, \Psi_n(\theta_0 + \epsilon) > \eta) = P(\theta_0 - \epsilon < \hat{\theta}_n < \theta_0 + \epsilon) + o(1) \]

    Choosing an appropriate threshold, let \(2\eta = \min\{-\Psi(\theta_0 - \epsilon), \Psi(\theta_0 + \epsilon)\} > 0\). By pointwise convergence, the probability on the left tends to 1, so the probability on the right also tends to 1, establishing consistency. \(\square\)

    3.2 Example: Applying Lemma 12.4 to Prove Consistency of the Median

    Sample median \(\hat{\theta}_n\) can be viewed as a zero of the equation \(\theta \mapsto \Psi_n(\theta) = n^{-1} \sum_{i=1}^n \text{sign}(X_i - \theta)\).

    By the Law of Large Numbers (LLN), for each fixed \(\theta\), we have:

    \[ \Psi_n(\theta) \xrightarrow{p} \Psi(\theta) = E[\text{sign}(X - \theta)] = P(X > \theta) - P(X < \theta) \]

    Now we verify the conditions of Lemma 12.4:

    • Check condition (a): The function \(\theta \mapsto \Psi_n(\theta)\) is clearly non-increasing (the lemma symmetrically applies to the non-increasing case by reversing the sign).

    • Check condition (b): We need to show \(-\Psi(\theta_0 - \epsilon) < 0 < -\Psi(\theta_0 + \epsilon)\). Examine the value of the limit function near \(\theta_0\):

    \[ \Psi(\theta_0 - \epsilon) = P(X > \theta_0 - \epsilon) - P(X < \theta_0 - \epsilon) = 1 - 2P(X < \theta_0 - \epsilon) \]

    At the population median \(\theta_0\), we have:

    \[ \Psi(\theta_0) = P(X > \theta_0) - P(X < \theta_0) = 0 \implies P(X > \theta_0) = P(X < \theta_0) = 0.5 \]

    Similarly:

    \[ \Psi(\theta_0 + \epsilon) = P(X > \theta_0 + \epsilon) - P(X < \theta_0 + \epsilon) = 1 - 2P(X < \theta_0 + \epsilon) \]

    If the distribution of \(X\) is continuous and the population median is uniquely determined, then we must have:

    \[ P(X < \theta_0 - \epsilon) < 0.5 < P(X < \theta_0 + \epsilon) \quad \text{for all } \epsilon > 0. \]

    Therefore, both conditions (a2) and (b) are satisfied. Applying Lemma 12.4, we directly conclude that the sample median converges consistently to the population median, i.e., \(\hat{\theta}_n \xrightarrow{p} \theta_0\). This avoids the need for complex empirical process theory.

    4. Wald Consistency of M-Estimators

    4.1 Semi-continuity

    Semi-continuity is a topological property weaker than continuity.

    • Upper semi-continuous (u.s.c.): A real-valued function \(f\) is said to be upper semi-continuous at \(x_0\) if:
    \[ \limsup_{x \rightarrow x_0} f(x) \le f(x_0) \]
    • Lower semi-continuous (l.s.c.): A real-valued function \(f\) is said to be lower semi-continuous at \(x_0\) if \(-f\) is upper semi-continuous, i.e.,
    \[ \liminf_{x \rightarrow x_0} f(x) \ge f(x_0) \]

    In statistical inference, upper semi-continuous M-functions are often used to establish Wald consistency conditions.

    4.2 Wald Consistency Conditions

    Let \(P m_\theta := E m_\theta(X)\). Typically, the map \(\theta \mapsto P m_\theta\) has a unique global maximum at \(\theta_0\). However, in the framework of Wald consistency, we allow multiple global maxima:

    \[ \Theta_0 := \{\theta_0 \in \Theta : P m_{\theta_0} = \sup_\theta P m_\theta\} \ne \emptyset \]

    Here, \(P m\) attains its maximum on the set \(\Theta_0\).

    Theorem 12.4 (Wald Consistency of M-Estimators)

    For every compact set \(K \subset \Theta\), the Wald consistency conditions ensuring \(P(d(\hat{\theta}_n, \Theta_0) \ge \epsilon, \hat{\theta}_n \in K) \rightarrow 0\) are:

    • W1. Upper semi-continuity condition: The map \(\theta \mapsto m_\theta(x)\) is upper semi-continuous for almost every \(x\).

    • W2. Uniform bound on small balls: For any small ball \(U \subset \Theta\), assume \(x \mapsto \sup_{\theta \in U} m_\theta(x)\) is measurable and satisfies the following dominated convergence (W-condition):

    \[ E \sup_{\theta \in U} m_\theta(X) < \infty \]
    • W3. Approximate maximization on compact sets: The sequence \(\{\hat{\theta}_n\}\) satisfies:
    \[ M_n(\hat{\theta}_n) \ge M_n(\theta_0) - o_P(1) \]

    for some \(\theta_0 \in \Theta_0\).

    Proof of Theorem 12.4 (click to expand)

    Step 1: Construct a cover

    Let \(B = \{\theta \in K : d(\theta, \Theta_0) \ge \epsilon\}\). Our goal is to show:

    \[ P\{\hat{\theta}_n \in B\} \rightarrow 0 \]

    If the function \(\theta \mapsto P m_\theta\) is identically \(-\infty\), then \(\Theta_0 = \Theta\) holds trivially. Hence, we may assume there exists \(\theta_0 \in \Theta_0\) such that \(E m_{\theta_0} > -\infty\). By condition W2, \(E|m_{\theta_0}(X)| < \infty\).

    Fix some \(\theta \in K\), and let \(U_l \ni \theta\) be a decreasing sequence of open balls containing \(\theta\) with diameters converging to 0. Write \(m_U(x) := \sup_{\theta \in U} m_\theta(x)\). Clearly, for each \(l\), the sequence:

    \[ m_\theta(x) \le m_{U_l}(x) = \sup_{\theta \in U_l} m_\theta(x) \]

    is decreasing in \(l\). Taking the limit gives:

    \[ m_\theta(x) \le \lim_{U_l \rightarrow \theta} m_{U_l}(x) \le m_\theta(x) \quad a.s. \]

    where the last inequality follows from the upper semi-continuity of \(m_\theta(x)\) (condition W1).

    Step 2: Apply the monotone convergence theorem

    Since \(m_{U_l} \downarrow m_\theta\) a.e., applying the monotone convergence theorem to \(\{-m_{U_l}\}\) yields:

    \[ E m_{U_l}(X) \rightarrow E m_\theta(X) \]

    Thus, for all \(\theta \notin \Theta_0\), because \(\theta_0\) maximizes \(E m_\theta(X)\), we must have:

    \[ E m_{U_l}(X) \rightarrow E m_\theta(X) < E m_{\theta_0}(X) \]

    Therefore, there exists a sufficiently small open ball \(U_k\) covering \(\theta\) such that:

    \[ E m_{U_k}(X) < E m_{\theta_0}(X), \quad \forall \theta \notin \Theta_0, \exists k \in \mathbb{N} \]

    By compactness of \(K\), the compact set \(B\) can be covered by finitely many such balls \(U_{\theta_1}, \dots, U_{\theta_p}\). Combining with the Law of Large Numbers (LLN), we have:

    \[ \sup_{\theta \in B} P_n m_\theta \le \max_{j=1,\dots,p} P_n m_{\theta_j} \le \max_{j=1,\dots,p} P_n \sup_{\theta \in U_{\theta_j}} m_\theta \]
    \[ \xrightarrow{a.s.} \max_{j=1,\dots,p} E \sup_{\theta \in U_{\theta_j}} m_\theta(X) < E m_{\theta_0}(X) \]

    Step 3: Probability measure limiting bound

    On the other hand, by the approximate maximization assumption, the event \(\{\hat{\theta}_n \in B\}\) implies the following event:

    \[ \sup_{\theta \in B} P_n m_\theta \ge P_n m_{\hat{\theta}_n} \ge P_n m_{\theta_0} - o_P(1) = E m_{\theta_0}(X) - o_P(1) \]

    where the equality follows from applying the law of large numbers to the single point \(P_n m_{\theta_0}\). Therefore, we have the event inclusion:

    \[ \{\hat{\theta}_n \in B\} \subseteq \left\{ \sup_{\theta \in B} P_n m_\theta \ge E m_{\theta_0}(X) - o_P(1) \right\} \]

    Consequently,

    \[ P\{\hat{\theta}_n \in B\} \le P\left\{ \sup_{\theta \in B} P_n m_\theta \ge E m_{\theta_0}(X) - o_P(1) \right\} \rightarrow 0 \]

    The proof is complete. \(\square\)

    4.3 Example: Location MLE for the Cauchy Distribution

    Consider the Cauchy distribution \(\text{Cauchy}(\theta)\), with probability density function (pdf):

    \[ f_\theta(x) = \frac{1}{\pi \{1 + (x - \theta)^2\}} \]

    The maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) for the location parameter \(\theta\) based on i.i.d. samples \(X_1, \dots, X_n \sim \text{Cauchy}(\theta)\) maximizes the log-likelihood function \(\theta \mapsto P_n m_\theta\):

    \[ m_\theta(x) = -\log(1 + (x - \theta)^2) \]

    Since the original parameter space \(\mathbb{R}\) is not compact, to apply Wald's theorem, we can extend the parameter space to its compactification:

    \[ \overline{\mathbb{R}} := \mathbb{R} \cup \{\pm\infty\} \]

    Now we verify the Wald consistency conditions one by one:

    • W1. Upper semi-continuity: Since \(m_\theta(x)\) is continuous on the entire extended space, the upper semi-continuity condition holds trivially.

    • W2. Uniform bound on small balls: For any open ball \(U\), we have:

    \[ E \sup_{\theta \in U} m_\theta(X) = \int \sup_{\theta \in U} \frac{-\log\{1 + (x - \theta)^2\}}{\pi \{1 + (x - \theta)^2\}} dx < \infty \]

    and the limit as \(\theta \rightarrow \pm\infty\) behaves well:

    \[ m_{-\infty}(x) = \lim_{\theta \rightarrow -\infty} \sup m_\theta(x) = -\infty \]
    \[ m_{\infty}(x) = \lim_{\theta \rightarrow \infty} \sup m_\theta(x) = -\infty \]
    • W3. Approximate maximization on compact sets: This holds by definition of the estimator.

    We equip \(\Theta = \overline{\mathbb{R}}\) with the following distance metric:

    \[ d(\theta_1, \theta_2) = |\arctan \theta_1 - \arctan \theta_2| \]

    By the identifiability condition of maximum likelihood, the set of maximizers is a singleton \(\Theta_0 = \{\theta_0\}\). Taking the compact set \(K = \overline{\mathbb{R}}\) and applying Wald's theorem directly yields \(d(\hat{\theta}_n, \Theta_0) \xrightarrow{p} 0\), i.e., the Cauchy MLE is consistent.

    5. Asymptotic Normality of Z-Estimators

    5.1 Theorem on Asymptotic Normality

    Theorem 12.5 (Asymptotic Normality of Z-Estimators)

    Assume the following standard conditions hold:

    • (C1) Measurability: For each \(\theta\) in the parameter space (an open subset of Euclidean space), the map \(x \mapsto \psi_\theta(x)\) is a measurable vector-valued function.

    • (C2) Local Lipschitz condition: For any \(\theta_1\) and \(\theta_2\) in a neighborhood of the true parameter \(\theta_0\), there exists a measurable envelope function \(\dot{\psi}(x)\) with \(P[\dot{\psi}(X)]^2 < \infty\) such that:

    \[ \|\psi_{\theta_1}(x) - \psi_{\theta_2}(x)\| \le \dot{\psi}(x) \|\theta_1 - \theta_2\| \]
    • (C3) Moment and differentiability conditions: Assume \(P\|\psi_{\theta_0}\|^2 < \infty\), and the map \(\theta \mapsto P \psi_\theta\) is differentiable at the zero \(\theta_0\), with non-singular derivative matrix \(V_{\theta_0}\):
    \[ V_{\theta_0} := \left. \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} P[\psi_\theta(X)] \right|_{\theta = \theta_0} \]
    • (C4) Consistency: The estimator satisfies \(P_n \psi_{\hat{\theta}_n} = o_P(n^{-1/2})\) and \(\hat{\theta}_n \xrightarrow{p} \theta_0\).

    Then the estimator satisfies asymptotic normality:

    \[ \sqrt{n}(\hat{\theta}_n - \theta_0) = -V_{\theta_0}^{-1} \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}} \sum_{i=1}^n \psi_{\theta_0}(X_i) + o_P(1) \xrightarrow{d} N\left(0, V_{\theta_0}^{-1} P \psi_{\theta_0} \psi_{\theta_0}^T (V_{\theta_0}^{-1})^T\right) \]

    5.2 Empirical Processes and Donsker Classes

    To rigorously prove the asymptotic normality of M-estimators and Z-estimators, we need tools from empirical process theory.

    • Basic setup: Let \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) be random samples from an unknown distribution \(P\) on a measurable space \((\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{A})\). Define the empirical distribution measure:
    \[ P_n = n^{-1} \sum_{i=1}^n \delta_{X_i} \]
    • Operator notation: For a measurable function \(f: \mathcal{X} \mapsto \mathbb{R}\), we denote the expectation under \(P_n\) and \(P\) respectively as:
    \[ P_n f = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n f(X_i), \quad P f = \int f dP \]
    • Empirical process linear functional: Define the empirical process acting on a function \(f\) as:
    \[ \mathbb{G}_n f = \sqrt{n}(P_n f - P f) = \sqrt{n}\left( \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n f(X_i) - E f(X) \right) \]

    Definition 12.4 (Donsker Class of Functions)

    A measurable function class \(\mathcal{F}\) is called a P-Donsker class if, for all \(f \in \mathcal{F}\), the sequence \(\mathbb{G}_n f\) converges weakly in \(\ell^\infty(\mathcal{F})\) (the space of bounded linear functionals) to a tight limiting Gaussian process.

    • Robust Lipschitz Donsker criterion: Let \(\mathcal{F} = \{f_\theta : \theta \in \Theta\}\) be a function class indexed by a bounded subset \(\Theta \subset \mathbb{R}^d\). If there exists a measurable function \(m(x)\) such that \(f_\theta(x)\) satisfies a global Lipschitz condition:
    \[ |f_{\theta_1}(x) - f_{\theta_2}(x)| \le m(x) \|\theta_1 - \theta_2\|, \quad \forall \theta_1, \theta_2 \in \Theta \]

    and if \(P|m|^r < \infty\) for some \(r > 0\), then \(\mathcal{F}\) is P-Donsker.

    5.3 Key Random Function Lemma

    Lemma 12.6 (van der Vaart Lemma 19.24)

    Assume the following conditions hold:

    • (a) \(\mathcal{F}\) is a P-Donsker measurable function class.

    • (b) \(\{\hat{f}_n\}\) is a sequence of random functions taking values in \(\mathcal{F}\) and converging in \(L_2(P)\) to some deterministic function \(f_0\):

    \[ \int (\hat{f}_n(x) - f_0(x))^2 dP(x) = P(\hat{f}_n - f_0)^2 \xrightarrow{p} 0 \]

    Then:

    \[ \mathbb{G}_n(\hat{f}_n - f_0) \xrightarrow{p} 0 \quad \text{and} \quad \mathbb{G}_n \hat{f}_n \xrightarrow{d} \mathbb{G}_P f_0 \]
    Extension of Lemma 12.6 to Z-Estimators (click to expand)

    In the framework of the theorem, we set \(\hat{f}_n := \psi_{\hat{\theta}_n}\) and \(f_0 := \psi_{\theta_0}\). By the Lipschitz condition:

    \[ P(\psi_{\hat{\theta}_n} - \psi_{\theta_0})^2 \le P(\dot{\psi})^2 \|\hat{\theta}_n - \theta_0\|^2 \rightarrow 0 \]

    When \(\hat{\theta}_n \xrightarrow{p} \theta_0\) and the envelope moment condition \(P(\dot{\psi})^2 < \infty\) holds, the above condition is satisfied. Therefore, because \(\mathcal{F} = \{\psi_\theta : \theta \in \Theta\}\) is P-Donsker, Lemma 12.6 directly implies:

    \[ \mathbb{G}_n(\hat{f}_n - f_0) = \sqrt{n}(P_n \psi_{\hat{\theta}_n} - P \psi_{\hat{\theta}_n}) - \sqrt{n}(P_n \psi_{\theta_0} - P \psi_{\theta_0}) \xrightarrow{p} 0 \]

    Rearranging gives:

    \[ \sqrt{n}(P_n \psi_{\hat{\theta}_n} - P \psi_{\hat{\theta}_n}) = \sqrt{n}(P_n \psi_{\theta_0} - P \psi_{\theta_0}) + o_P(1) \]

    The proof is complete. \(\square\)

    5.4 Complete Proof of Theorem 12.5

    Asymptotic expansion proof of Theorem 12.5 (click to expand)

    From the derivation in Section 5.3, we have established the asymptotic equivalence of the empirical process:

    \[ \mathbb{G}_n \psi_{\hat{\theta}_n} - \mathbb{G}_n \psi_{\theta_0} = \mathbb{G}_n(\psi_{\hat{\theta}_n} - \psi_{\theta_0}) \xrightarrow{p} 0 \quad \text{--- (12.13)} \]

    Meanwhile, expand the definition of the empirical process: \(\mathbb{G}_n \psi_{\hat{\theta}_n} = \sqrt{n}(P_n \psi_{\hat{\theta}_n} - P \psi_{\hat{\theta}_n})\). Since \(\theta_0\) is the true population zero satisfying \(P \psi_{\theta_0} = 0\), we can rewrite the above as:

    \[ \mathbb{G}_n \psi_{\hat{\theta}_n} = \sqrt{n}(P_n \psi_{\hat{\theta}_n} - P \psi_{\hat{\theta}_n}) = \sqrt{n} P_n \psi_{\hat{\theta}_n} + \sqrt{n}(P \psi_{\theta_0} - P \psi_{\hat{\theta}_n}) \]

    By the approximate zero condition (C4), \(\sqrt{n} P_n \psi_{\hat{\theta}_n} = o_P(1)\). Substituting this yields:

    \[ \mathbb{G}_n \psi_{\hat{\theta}_n} = \sqrt{n}(P \psi_{\theta_0} - P \psi_{\hat{\theta}_n}) + o_P(1) \quad \text{--- (12.14)} \]

    Combining (12.14) with (12.13), we can eliminate the empirical linear term at the estimator location:

    \[ \mathbb{G}_n \psi_{\theta_0} = \mathbb{G}_n \psi_{\hat{\theta}_n} + o_P(1) = \sqrt{n}(P \psi_{\theta_0} - P \psi_{\hat{\theta}_n}) + o_P(1) \]

    Next, perform a first-order Taylor expansion of the deterministic expectation function \(P \psi_\theta\) around \(\theta_0\):

    \[ \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}} \sum_{i=1}^n \psi_{\theta_0}(X_i) + o_P(1) = -\sqrt{n} \left. \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} P \psi_\theta(X) \right|_{\theta = \theta_0} (\hat{\theta}_n - \theta_0) + \sqrt{n} o_P(\|\hat{\theta}_n - \theta_0\|) \]

    Substituting the matrix \(V_{\theta_0}\):

    \[ \sqrt{n} V_{\theta_0}(\hat{\theta}_n - \theta_0) = -\mathbb{G}_n \psi_{\theta_0} + \sqrt{n} o_P(\|\hat{\theta}_n - \theta_0\|) \quad \text{--- (12.15)} \]

    Because the central limit theorem (CLT) guarantees \(\mathbb{G}_n \psi_{\theta_0} = O_P(1)\), equation (12.15) implies stochastic boundedness: \(\sqrt{n} \|V_{\theta_0}(\hat{\theta}_n - \theta_0)\| = O_P(1)\). Using non-singularity of the matrix:

    \[ \sqrt{n} \|\hat{\theta}_n - \theta_0\| \le \|V_{\theta_0}^{-1}\| \sqrt{n} \|V_{\theta_0}(\hat{\theta}_n - \theta_0)\| = O_P(1) + o_P(\sqrt{n} \|\hat{\theta}_n - \theta_0\|) \]

    This proves the \(\sqrt{n}\)-consistency, i.e., \(\sqrt{n} \|\hat{\theta}_n - \theta_0\| = O_P(1)\).

    Substituting this rate back into the remainder term in (12.15), the higher-order term becomes \(o_P(1)\):

    \[ \sqrt{n}(\hat{\theta}_n - \theta_0) = -V_{\theta_0}^{-1} \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}} \sum_{i=1}^n \psi_{\theta_0}(X_i) + o_P(1) \]

    Finally, applying the multivariate central limit theorem and Slutsky's theorem yields the asymptotic normality. \(\square\)

    6. Asymptotic Normality of M-Estimators

    Analogous to Z-estimators, we can establish asymptotic normality of M-estimators by imposing smoothness and differentiability conditions on the criterion function.

    Theorem 12.6 (Asymptotic Normality of M-Estimators)

    Assume the following standard conditions hold:

    • Measurability: For each \(\theta\) in the open parameter space, the map \(x \mapsto m_\theta(x)\) is measurable, and \(\theta \mapsto m_\theta(x)\) is differentiable at the true point \(\theta_0\) for \(P\)-almost every \(x\), with first derivative (score function) denoted \(\dot{m}_{\theta_0}(x)\).

    • Lipschitz condition: There exists a square-integrable control function \(\dot{m}(x)\) (\(P[\dot{m}(X)]^2 < \infty\)) such that in a neighborhood of \(\theta_0\):

    \[ |m_{\theta_1}(x) - m_{\theta_2}(x)| \le \dot{m}(x) \|\theta_1 - \theta_2\| \]
    • Moment and second-order differentiability: Assume the map \(\theta \mapsto P m_\theta\) admits the following second-order Taylor expansion at the global maximizer \(\theta_0\):
    \[ P m_\theta = P m_{\theta_0} + \frac{1}{2}(\theta - \theta_0)^T V_{\theta_0}(\theta - \theta_0) + o(\|\theta - \theta_0\|^2) \]

    where the symmetric Hessian matrix \(V_{\theta_0}\) is non-singular:

    \[ V_{\theta_0} = \left. \frac{\partial^2}{\partial \theta^2} P[m_\theta(X)] \right|_{\theta = \theta_0} \]
    • Consistency and approximate maximization rate: The estimator satisfies \(P_n m_{\hat{\theta}_n} \ge \sup_\theta P_n m_\theta - o_P(n^{-1})\) and \(\hat{\theta}_n \xrightarrow{p} \theta_0\).

    Then the M-estimator satisfies asymptotic normality:

    \[ \sqrt{n}(\hat{\theta}_n - \theta_0) = -V_{\theta_0}^{-1} \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}} \sum_{i=1}^n \dot{m}_{\theta_0}(X_i) + o_P(1) \xrightarrow{d} N\left(0, V_{\theta_0}^{-1} P \dot{m}_{\theta_0} \dot{m}_{\theta_0}^T V_{\theta_0}^{-1}\right) \]

    7. Classical Application Examples

    7.1 Asymptotic Distribution of the Sample Median (via M-Estimation Theory)

    The sample median can be obtained by maximizing the following criterion function:

    \[ \theta \mapsto -\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n |X_i - \theta| \]

    Assume the population distribution function \(F(x)\) is differentiable at the true population median \(\theta_0 = F^{-1}(1/2)\), and its density satisfies \(f(\theta_0) > 0\).

    We use the centered M-estimation function: \(m_\theta(x) = |x - \theta| - |x|\). By the triangle inequality, for any \(\theta_1, \theta_2\):

    \[ ||x - \theta_1| - |x - \theta_2|| \le |\theta_1 - \theta_2| \]

    This shows the function satisfies a Lipschitz condition controlled by a constant, where \(\dot{m}(x) \equiv 1\), which obviously satisfies the square-integrability condition.

    Moreover, this map is differentiable almost everywhere except at \(x = \theta\), with first derivative (score function):

    \[ \dot{m}_{\theta_0}(x) = -\text{sign}(x - \theta_0) \]

    Its squared expectation is:

    \[ E(\dot{m}_{\theta_0}(X))^2 = E(\text{sign}^2(X - \theta_0)) = 1 \]

    Using integration by parts, we can derive the analytic form of the deterministic objective function:

    \[ P m_\theta = E(m_\theta(X)) = \int |x - \theta| dF(x) - \int |x| dF(x) \]
    \[ = \theta F(0) + \int_{(0, \theta]} 2(\theta - x) dF(x) - \theta(1 - F(0)) = 2\int_0^\theta F(x) dx - \theta \]

    Taking first and second derivatives of the objective function:

    \[ \frac{d P m_\theta}{d \theta} = 2F(\theta) - 1, \quad \frac{d^2 P m_\theta}{d \theta^2} = 2f(\theta) \]

    Therefore, the second-order matrix evaluated at \(\theta_0\) is \(V_{\theta_0} = 2f(\theta_0)\).

    Since the asymptotic variance takes the sandwich form \(V_{\theta_0}^{-1} P[\dot{m}_{\theta_0} \dot{m}_{\theta_0}^T] V_{\theta_0}^{-1} = \frac{1}{(2f(\theta_0)) \cdot 1 \cdot (2f(\theta_0))} = \frac{1}{4f^2(\theta_0)}\), substituting into Theorem 12.6 directly yields the classic asymptotic normality result for the median:

    \[ \sqrt{n}(\hat{\theta}_n - \theta_0) \xrightarrow{d} N\left(0, \frac{1}{4f^2(\theta_0)}\right) \]

    7.2 Nonlinear Least Squares Estimation

    Suppose observed samples \((X_1, Y_1), \dots, (X_n, Y_n)\) consist of covariates \(X\) and response \(Y\), with data generating mechanism:

    \[ Y = f_{\theta_0}(X) + e, \quad E(e|X) = 0, \quad \text{Var}(e|X) = \sigma^2(X) < \infty \]

    The nonlinear least squares estimator (LSE) minimizes the sum of squared residuals:

    \[ \hat{\theta}_n = \arg\min_\theta \sum_{i=1}^n (Y_i - f_\theta(X_i))^2 \]

    This is equivalent to a special M-estimator with loss function \(m_\theta(x, y) = -(y - f_\theta(x))^2\). The limit of the empirical objective function under the law of large numbers is:

    \[ \theta \mapsto P m_\theta = -E(Y - f_\theta(X))^2 = -E[Y - f_{\theta_0}(X) + (f_{\theta_0}(X) - f_\theta(X))]^2 \]
    \[ = -E(f_{\theta_0}(X) - f_\theta(X))^2 - E e^2 \quad \text{--- (12.16)} \]

    From (12.16), if the model satisfies an identifiability condition (i.e., \(\theta \ne \theta_0 \implies P(f_\theta(X) \ne f_{\theta_0}(X)) > 0\)), then the true parameter \(\theta_0\) is indeed a global maximizer of the objective function, guaranteeing consistency of the estimator.

    Now we verify the key Lipschitz condition for asymptotic normality:

    \[ |m_{\theta_1}(x,y) - m_{\theta_2}(x,y)| = |(y - f_{\theta_1}(x))^2 - (y - f_{\theta_2}(x))^2| \]
    \[ = |f_{\theta_1}(x) - f_{\theta_2}(x)| \cdot |2y - f_{\theta_1}(x) - f_{\theta_2}(x)| \]

    Assume the regression function is well-behaved in first order: \(|f_{\theta_1}(x) - f_{\theta_2}(x)| \le g(x) \|\theta_2 - \theta_1\|\), and the function values are uniformly bounded \(|f_\theta(x)| \le c(x)\). Then we can construct a square-integrable global Lipschitz envelope:

    \[ \dot{m}(x,y) := g(x)[2|y| + 2c(x)] \]

    Perform a Taylor expansion of the objective function (12.16) to obtain the Hessian matrix \(V_{\theta_0}\):

    \[ P m_\theta = P m_{\theta_0} - \int [f_\theta(x) - f_{\theta_0}(x)]^2 \rho(x) dx \]
    \[ = P m_{\theta_0} - \int [(\theta - \theta_0)^T \dot{f}_{\theta_0}(x) + o(\|\theta - \theta_0\|)]^2 \rho(x) dx \]
    \[ = P m_{\theta_0} - \frac{1}{2}(\theta - \theta_0)^T \left[ 2 \int \dot{f}_{\theta_0}(x) \dot{f}_{\theta_0}^T(x) p(x) dx \right] (\theta - \theta_0) + o(\|\theta - \theta_0\|^2) \]

    Thus, the Hessian matrix and the corresponding first-order score function are:

    \[ V_{\theta_0} = -2 P(\dot{f}_{\theta_0} \dot{f}_{\theta_0}^T), \quad \dot{m}_{\theta_0}(x,y) = 2(y - f_{\theta_0}(x)) \dot{f}_{\theta_0}(x) = 2 e \dot{f}_{\theta_0}(x) \]

    Plugging into the asymptotic normality formula for M-estimators, under the assumption of independence between the error \(e\) and covariates \(X\), the middle sandwich matrix simplifies to:

    \[ V_{\theta_0}^{-1} P(\dot{m}_{\theta_0} \dot{m}_{\theta_0}^T) V_{\theta_0}^{-1} = [2P \dot{f}_{\theta_0} \dot{f}_{\theta_0}^T]^{-1} \cdot 4 P(e^2 \dot{f}_{\theta_0} \dot{f}_{\theta_0}^T) \cdot [2P \dot{f}_{\theta_0} \dot{f}_{\theta_0}^T]^{-1} \]
    \[ = [P \dot{f}_{\theta_0} \dot{f}_{\theta_0}^T]^{-1} E(\sigma^2(X)) [P \dot{f}_{\theta_0} \dot{f}_{\theta_0}^T]^{-1} \]

    This determines the asymptotic distribution of the nonlinear least squares estimator.

    7.3 Binary Regression Model

    Suppose observed samples \((X_1, Y_1), \dots, (X_n, Y_n)\), where \(X_i\) is a \(k\)-dimensional covariate vector and \(Y_i \in \{0, 1\}\) is a binary response variable, with conditional probability model:

    \[ P_\theta(Y_i = 1 | X_i = x) = \Psi(\theta^T x) \]

    Here \(\Psi: \mathbb{R} \mapsto [0, 1]\) is a known continuously differentiable monotonic link function.

    • Logistic Regression: Take \(\Psi(t) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-t}}\).

    • Probit Model: Take \(\Psi(t) = \Phi(t)\) (standard normal distribution function).

    The maximum likelihood estimator for this model maximizes the conditional log-likelihood function:

    \[ \theta \mapsto \prod_{i=1}^n p_\theta(Y_i | X_i) := \prod_{i=1}^n \Psi(\theta^T X_i)^{Y_i} (1 - \Psi(\theta^T X_i))^{1 - Y_i} \]

    To ensure identifiability of the parameter, we require that the covariates are non-degenerate, i.e., the distribution of \(X_i\) does not concentrate on any \((k-1)\)-dimensional affine subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^k\). To this end, we assume the range of \(X_i\) is bounded and the matrix \(E X X^T\) is non-singular.

    Differentiating the log-likelihood gives the corresponding Z-estimator score equation:

    \[ \psi_\theta(x, y) := \dot{l}_\theta(y|x) = \frac{y - \Psi(\theta^T x)}{\Psi(\theta^T x)[1 - \Psi(\theta^T x)]} \Psi'(\theta^T x) x \]

    When the parameter \(\theta\) varies over a compact set, this score function is uniformly bounded in \((x,y)\), so the conditions are naturally satisfied. Compute the corresponding Fisher information matrix:

    \[ I_\theta = E \left[ \frac{\Psi'(\theta^T X)^2}{\Psi(\theta^T X)[1 - \Psi(\theta^T X)]} X X^T \right] \]

    Applying the asymptotic normality theorem for Z-estimators, the maximum likelihood estimator converges to the following normal limiting distribution:

    \[ \sqrt{n}(\hat{\theta}_n - \theta) \xrightarrow{d} N(0, I_\theta^{-1}) \]
