# Chapter 13: U-statistics (Part 1)

## 1. Why Study U-statistics?

### 1.1 Core Idea

In statistics, many estimands of interest are not expectations of a single observation, but rather **expectations of a function of several independent observations**:

$$
\theta(P) = E h(X_1, \dots, X_m), \quad X_i \stackrel{iid}{\sim} P
$$

A **U-statistic** estimates this quantity by averaging the function $h$ over all distinct $m$-tuples in the sample.

**Applications:**

* **Classical statistics**: Unbiased sample variance, Gini mean difference, Kendall's tau, Mann-Whitney/Wilcoxon statistic.

* **Statistical inference**: Asymptotic variance estimation, confidence intervals, permutation tests, rank tests.

* **Modern machine learning**: AUC/ranking problems, kernel two-sample tests based on MMD, independence tests based on HSIC, scalable incomplete U-statistics.

### 1.2 Intuition: Averaging over all subsamples

Suppose we have a simple unbiased estimator that uses only $m$ observations:

$$
h(X_1, \dots, X_m), \quad E h(X_1, \dots, X_m) = \theta
$$

In a sample of size $n$, there are many subsets of size $m$. Instead of choosing just one subset, we average over **all** such subsets:

$$
U_n = \binom{n}{m}^{-1} \sum_{1 \le i_1 < \dots < i_m \le n} h(X_{i_1}, \dots, X_{i_m})
$$

**Intuitive explanation:**
Given an unordered sample, averaging the naive estimator over all possible relabelings removes the artificial randomness introduced by choosing a particular subsample. This typically **reduces variance while maintaining unbiasedness**.

---

### 1.3 Example: Sample variance

Variance can be written as a two-sample functional:

$$
\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{2} E(X_1 - X_2)^2
$$

Thus, the unbiased sample variance can be expressed as:

$$
\binom{n}{2}^{-1} \sum_{1 \le i < j \le n} \frac{(X_i - X_j)^2}{2} = \frac{1}{n-1} \sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar{X}_n)^2
$$

* Our commonly used formula for sample variance is not an ad hoc formula.
* It is actually the **complete average** of the unbiased estimator $\frac{(X_1 - X_2)^2}{2}$ based on two observations.

## 2. The Principles of Mathematical Statistics Behind U-statistics

The original reason for introducing U-statistics was not just "stability through averaging"; it is a classic construction in mathematical statistics.

### 2.1 Starting Point: Estimable functional

If there exists a measurable function $h$ such that:

$$
\theta(P) = E_P h(X_1, \dots, X_m)
$$

where $X_1, \dots, X_m \stackrel{iid}{\sim} P$, then the parameter $\theta = \theta(P)$ is called an **estimable functional of degree $m$**.
In this case, $h(X_1, \dots, X_m)$ is the naive unbiased estimator of $\theta$ based on only $m$ observations.

* If $m=1$, the most natural estimator is the sample mean of $h(X_i)$.
* If $m \ge 2$, the sample contains many equally valid $m$-subsamples.
* The U-statistic is essentially the **Rao-Blackwellized** version of the naive estimator, removing the arbitrariness of subsample selection.

### 2.2 Examples of Estimable Functionals

| Degree | Functional $\theta(P)$ | Unbiased kernel $h$ | Interpretation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | $\mu = EX$ | $h(x) = x$ | Many nonlinear parameters are essentially expectations, but expectations based on several independent copies. |
| 1 | $EX^r$ | $h(x) = x^r$ | U-statistics are the natural unbiased extension of sample means for such functionals. |
| 2 | $\mu^2$ | $h(x_1, x_2) = x_1 x_2$ | |
| 2 | $\sigma^2$ | $h(x_1, x_2) = (x_1 - x_2)^2 / 2$ | |
| 2 | $F(t)^2$ | $h(x_1, x_2) = \mathbb{I}(x_1 \le t)\mathbb{I}(x_2 \le t)$ | |
| 2 | $\mathbb{P}(X < Y)$ | $h(x, y) = \mathbb{I}(x < y)$ | Two-sample |
| 2 | Gini mean difference | $h(x, y) = |x - y|$ | |

## 3. Sufficient Statistics and the Rao-Blackwell Principle

### 3.1 Unordered Sample as a Sufficient Statistic

Let the order statistic (or, when ties may exist, the unordered sample/empirical measure $P_n$) be:

$$
T = (X_{(1)}, \dots, X_{(n)})
$$

* For i.i.d. samples from any distribution $P$, the unordered sample $T$ is **sufficient** for $P$.
* Equivalently, given $T$, the remaining randomness is just the random assignment of labels to the observations.
* This conditional label distribution is **free of $P$**: all label permutations are equally likely (accounting for multiplicities if ties exist).

### 3.2 Rao-Blackwell Principle

If $A$ is an unbiased estimator, then the conditional expectation $E(A|T)$ remains unbiased, and:

$$
Var\{E(A|T)\} \le Var(A)
$$

Thus, conditioning on the unordered sample removes label noise without changing the target of estimation.

---

## 4. Key Identity: Conditional Expectation Constructs the U-statistic

Start from the naive unbiased estimator:

$$
A = h(X_1, \dots, X_m), \quad E A = \theta
$$

Given the unordered sample $T$, the first $m$ labels are drawn uniformly without replacement from $\{1, \dots, n\}$. Therefore:

$$
E\{h(X_1, \dots, X_m) | T\} = \frac{(n-m)!}{n!} \sum_{(i_1, \dots, i_m): \text{distinct}} h(X_{i_1}, \dots, X_{i_m})
$$

$$
= \binom{n}{m}^{-1} \sum_{1 \le i_1 < \dots < i_m \le n} h^{sym}(X_{i_1}, \dots, X_{i_m})
$$

If the kernel $h$ is symmetric, then this is exactly the U-statistic:

$$
U_n = \binom{n}{m}^{-1} \sum_{1 \le i_1 < \dots < i_m \le n} h(X_{i_1}, \dots, X_{i_m})
$$

### 4.1 Implications of the Rao-Blackwell Construction

The identity above provides a finite-sample explanation of why "complete averaging" is the canonical estimator.

1.  **Unbiasedness**:

    $$
    E U_n = E\{E(A|T)\} = E A = \theta
    $$

2.  **Variance reduction relative to a single subsample**:

    $$
    Var(U_n) = Var\{E(A|T)\} \le Var(A)
    $$

3.  **Connection to UMVU theory**:
    In parametric models, if $T$ is complete and sufficient, then by the Lehmann-Scheffé theorem, the same conditional expectation is the **uniformly minimum variance unbiased estimator (UMVU)**.

**What remains nontrivial:**
The terms in the sum of $U_n$ are **dependent** due to overlap between different subsamples. The subsequent sections of this chapter develop projection and overlap tools to quantify this dependence.

## 5. In-depth Classic Examples

### 5.1 Example: Deriving sample variance via Rao-Blackwellization

For $\theta = \sigma^2$, the unbiased estimator based on two observations is:

$$
A = \frac{(X_1 - X_2)^2}{2}, \quad E A = \frac{1}{2} E(X_1 - X_2)^2 = \sigma^2
$$

Conditioning on the unordered sample $T$ yields:

$$
E(A|T) = \binom{n}{2}^{-1} \sum_{1 \le i < j \le n} \frac{(X_i - X_j)^2}{2} = \frac{1}{n-1} \sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar{X}_n)^2
$$

Thus, the familiar unbiased sample variance is the Rao-Blackwellized version of the naive two-point estimator.

> **Note**: The formula with denominator $n-1$ is not an empirical correction trick; it is a natural consequence of averaging over all data pairs.

### 5.2 Example: Removing the diagonal bias

Fix $t \in \mathbb{R}$ and let $p = F(t)$. The parameter $p^2$ has the following unbiased two-sample kernel:

$$
h(x_1, x_2) = \mathbb{I}(x_1 \le t)\mathbb{I}(x_2 \le t)
$$

The corresponding U-statistic is:

$$
U_n(t) = \frac{1}{n(n-1)} \sum_{i \ne j} \mathbb{I}(X_i \le t)\mathbb{I}(X_j \le t)
$$

If we let $N_t = \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{I}(X_i \le t)$, then:

$$
U_n(t) = \frac{N_t(N_t - 1)}{n(n-1)}, \quad E U_n(t) = p^2
$$

In contrast, the traditional plug-in estimator includes the diagonal terms and satisfies:

$$
F_n(t)^2 = (N_t / n)^2
$$

$$
E F_n(t)^2 = p^2 + \frac{p(1-p)}{n}
$$

(with a bias of $O(1/n)$).

## 6. From Intuition to Asymptotic Theory

The Rao-Blackwell picture explains the **construction** of U-statistics; the projection picture will explain their **asymptotic properties**.

* Reveals how the conditional expectation of the kernel changes with one observation (first-order effect):

    $$
    h_1(x) - \theta = E\{h(x, X_2, \dots, X_m)\} - \theta
    $$

    If the variance of this first-order effect is positive, then many weakly dependent overlapping terms sum to a normal limit:

    $$
    \sqrt{n}(U_n - \theta) \approx \frac{m}{\sqrt{n}} \sum_{i=1}^n \{h_1(X_i) - \theta\}
    $$

* If the first-order effect is zero, the statistic is **degenerate**, and higher-order interactions determine its convergence rate and limiting distribution.

**Summary**: U-statistics emerge from sufficiency and Rao-Blackwellization, but their analysis relies primarily on Hoeffding's projection.

## 7. Formal Definitions: Symmetric Kernels and U-statistics

Assume $X_1, \dots, X_n \stackrel{iid}{\sim} P$ on a measurable space $(\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{A})$, and fix $m < n$.

!!! info "Definition 9.1 (Symmetric kernel)"

    A measurable function $h: \mathcal{X}^m \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ is called a symmetric kernel of order $m$ if for any permutation $\pi$ of $\{1, \dots, m\}$,

    $$
    h(x_1, \dots, x_m) = h(x_{\pi(1)}, \dots, x_{\pi(m)})
    $$

If $h$ is not symmetric, it can be replaced by its **symmetrization**:

$$
h^{sym}(x_1, \dots, x_m) = \frac{1}{m!} \sum_{\pi} h(x_{\pi(1)}, \dots, x_{\pi(m)})
$$

If the original statistic averages over unordered subsets, the U-statistic remains unchanged after symmetrization.

!!! info "Definition 9.2 (U-statistic)"

    Assume $E|h(X_1, \dots, X_m)| < \infty$ and define:

    $$
    \theta = \theta(P) = E h(X_1, \dots, X_m)
    $$

    The U-statistic based on the kernel $h$ of order $m$ is defined as:

    $$
    U_n = \binom{n}{m}^{-1} \sum_{1 \le i_1 < \dots < i_m \le n} h(X_{i_1}, \dots, X_{i_m})
    $$

Clearly, $E U_n = \theta$. The "U" stands for Unbiasedness: the expectation of each term is $\theta$.

## 8. Relationship with Plug-in and V-statistics

The empirical distribution can be written as $P_n = n^{-1} \sum_{i=1}^n \delta_{X_i}$. A natural plug-in estimator is the **V-statistic**:

$$
V_n = \int h(x_1, \dots, x_m) dP_n(x_1) \dots dP_n(x_m) = \frac{1}{n^m} \sum_{i_1, \dots, i_m = 1}^n h(X_{i_1}, \dots, X_{i_m})
$$

* $V_n$ includes repeated indices, so it may be biased for finite $n$.
* $U_n$ removes repeated indices and is strictly unbiased.
* For fixed $m$ and regular kernels, the difference between $U_n$ and $V_n$ is typically $O_p(n^{-1})$, but this difference can be important when dealing with degenerate limits.

## 9. Core Examples in Modern and Classical Statistics

### 9.1 Rank Example: Kendall's tau

!!! example "Example 9.3 (Kendall's tau)"

    For bivariate data $Z_i = (X_i, Y_i)$, the parameter of interest is:

    \[
    \tau = E[\text{sign}\{(X_1 - X_2)(Y_1 - Y_2)\}]
    \]

    The corresponding kernel is:

    \[
    h(z_1, z_2) = \text{sign}\{(x_1 - x_2)(y_1 - y_2)\}
    \]

    The resulting U-statistic estimator is:

    \[
    \hat{\tau} = \binom{n}{2}^{-1} \sum_{i < j} h(Z_i, Z_j)
    \]

    It is often used to measure monotone association between variables.

### 9.2 Classic Test: Mann-Whitney and AUC

!!! example "Example 9.4 (Mann-Whitney parameter and AUC)"

    For two independent samples $X_1, \dots, X_n \sim P$ and $Y_1, \dots, Y_N \sim Q$, the parameter is defined as:

    \[
    \theta = \mathbb{P}(X > Y) + \frac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}(X = Y)
    \]

    This parameter can be estimated by a two-sample U-statistic:

    \[
    U_{n,N} = \frac{1}{nN} \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^N \left\{ \mathbb{I}(X_i > Y_j) + \frac{1}{2}\mathbb{I}(X_i = Y_j) \right\}
    \]

    In machine learning, this corresponds to the population AUC (Area Under the ROC Curve) for a scoring rule.

### 9.3 Kernel Two-sample Test: MMD

Let $X, X' \sim P$ and $Y, Y' \sim Q$ be independent, and let $k$ be a positive definite kernel.
The squared Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) is defined as:

\[
\text{MMD}^2(P, Q) = E k(X, X') + E k(Y, Y') - 2 E k(X, Y)
\]

For paired observations $Z_i = (X_i, Y_i)$, its unbiased estimator is a second-order U-statistic:

\[
\widehat{\text{MMD}}_u^2 = \binom{n}{2}^{-1} \sum_{i < j} h(Z_i, Z_j)
\]

where the symmetric kernel is:

\[
h(z_i, z_j) = k(x_i, x_j) + k(y_i, y_j) - k(x_i, y_j) - k(x_j, y_i)
\]

* **Property**: Under the null hypothesis $H_0: P = Q$, the **first-order projection of this kernel is zero**. Therefore, the statistic is **degenerate**, and its null limiting distribution is a weighted chi-square type.

### 9.4 Kernel Independence Test: HSIC

Let $Z = (X, Y)$, and let $k$ and $l$ be kernels on the spaces of $X$ and $Y$, respectively. The Hilbert-Schmidt Independence Criterion (HSIC) satisfies: for characteristic kernels,

\[
\text{HSIC}(P_{XY}, k, l) = 0 \quad \text{if and only if } X \text{ and } Y \text{ are independent}
\]

* The unbiased empirical HSIC is a **U-statistic of order 4**.
* Under the independence assumption, it is **degenerate**: its first-order projection vanishes.
* This explains why its null distribution is not a simple normal limit; in practice, spectral methods, permutation tests, or wild-bootstrap are used for approximation.

**Key takeaway:** Degenerate U-statistics are not theoretical curiosities; they are at the absolute core of modern kernel tests.

## 10. Conditional Projections and Hoeffding's Variance Formula

To quantify the dependence among cross-terms in a U-statistic, we introduce conditional projection tools.

### 10.1 Conditional Projections

Assume the kernel has finite second moment: $E h^2(X_1, \dots, X_m) < \infty$.
For $k = 0, 1, \dots, m$, define the conditional expectation projection:

* For $k=0$:

\[
h_0 = \theta
\]

* For $1 \le k \le m$:

\[
h_k(x_1, \dots, x_k) = E\{h(x_1, \dots, x_k, X_{k+1}, \dots, X_m)\}
\]

Note that $h_m = h$, and the expectation satisfies:

\[
E h_k(X_1, \dots, X_k) = \theta
\]

Next, define the **centered projection**:

\[
\tilde{h}_k(x_1, \dots, x_k) = h_k(x_1, \dots, x_k) - \theta
\]

and the corresponding projection variance:

\[
\xi_k = Var\{h_k(X_1, \dots, X_k)\}
\]

### 10.2 Monotonicity of the Projection Variances

By the tower property of conditional expectation:

\[
h_k(X_1, \dots, X_k) = E\{h_{k+1}(X_1, \dots, X_{k+1}) | X_1, \dots, X_k\}
\]

Applying Jensen's inequality yields:

\[
\xi_k \le \xi_{k+1}
\]

where $\xi_0 = 0$ and $\xi_m = Var\{h(X_1, \dots, X_m)\}$.

* **Intuition**: For $k = 0, 1, \dots, m-1$, the projection variance $\xi_k$ measures how much of the total variation of the kernel is already explained by knowing $k$ of its $m$ arguments.

### 10.3 Hoeffding's Variance Formula

!!! success "Theorem 9.5 (Hoeffding's variance formula)"

    If $E h^2(X_1, \dots, X_m) < \infty$, then the exact variance of the U-statistic is:

    \[
    Var(U_n) = \binom{n}{m}^{-1} \sum_{k=1}^m \binom{m}{k} \binom{n-m}{m-k} \xi_k
    \]

    Moreover, it satisfies the following important properties:

    * **1. Upper and lower bounds**:

    \[
    \frac{m^2}{n} \xi_1 \le Var(U_n) \le \frac{m}{n} \xi_m
    \]

    * **2. Monotonicity in sample size**:

    \[
    (n+1)Var(U_{n+1}) \le n Var(U_n)
    \]

    * **3. Asymptotic variance under degeneracy**:
    If there exists an integer $r$ such that $\xi_1 = \dots = \xi_{r-1} = 0$ and $\xi_r > 0$, then:

    \[
    Var(U_n) = \frac{r! \binom{m}{r}^2 \xi_r}{n^r} + O(n^{-(r+1)})
    \]

??? proof "Proof of Theorem 9.5 (click to expand)"

    **Step 1: Computing covariance by overlap**

    Let $\tilde{h} = h - \theta$ be the centered kernel. If index subsets $I$ and $J$ have exactly $k$ common elements (i.e., $|I \cap J| = k$), then conditioning on those $k$ common observations yields:

    \[
    E\{\tilde{h}(X_I)\tilde{h}(X_J)\} = E \left[ E\{\tilde{h}(X_I) | X_{I \cap J}\} E\{\tilde{h}(X_J) | X_{I \cap J}\} \right]
    \]

    By the definition of projections, this equals:

    \[
    = E\{\tilde{h}_k(X_1, \dots, X_k)^2\} = \xi_k
    \]

    This is the most crucial step: **the covariance depends only on the number $k$ of shared indices** between the two subsets.

    **Step 2: Counting overlaps**

    Fix a particular subset $I$. We need to count how many subsets $J$ of size $m$ share exactly $k$ elements with $I$.
    Combinatorially, this is equivalent to choosing $k$ elements from the $m$ elements of $I$, and $m-k$ elements from the $n-m$ elements not in $I$:

    \[
    \binom{m}{k}\binom{n-m}{m-k}
    \]

    Expanding the variance as a double sum:

    \[
    Var(U_n) = \binom{n}{m}^{-2} \sum_{I} \sum_{J} Cov\{h(X_I), h(X_J)\}
    \]

    Grouping terms:

    \[
    = \binom{n}{m}^{-2} \sum_{I} \sum_{k=1}^m \binom{m}{k}\binom{n-m}{m-k} \xi_k
    \]

    The outer sum over $I$ has $\binom{n}{m}$ terms, hence:

    \[
    = \binom{n}{m}^{-1} \sum_{k=1}^m \binom{m}{k}\binom{n-m}{m-k} \xi_k
    \]

    (Note: when $k=0$, the two subsets are completely independent, covariance is 0, and the corresponding term vanishes.) This completes the proof. $\square$

### 10.4 Rates: Why Degeneracy Matters

Analyzing the coefficients of $\xi_k$ in the variance formula, we can perform an asymptotic expansion:

\[
\binom{n}{m}^{-1}\binom{m}{k}\binom{n-m}{m-k} = \binom{m}{k}^2 k! n^{-k} + O(n^{-(k+1)})
\]

Thus, **the first non-zero projection variance determines the stochastic order of the U-statistic**.

If $\xi_1 = \dots = \xi_{r-1} = 0$ and $\xi_r > 0$, then the fluctuation is:

\[
U_n - \theta = O_p(n^{-r/2})
\]

* When **$r = 1$**: The U-statistic is **non-degenerate**, with $\sqrt{n}$ rate and normal limit distribution.
* When **$r = 2$**: The U-statistic is **degenerate of order 2**, with a faster convergence rate ($n^{-1}$), and the limit distribution is non-Gaussian (typically a weighted sum of infinitely many chi-square random variables).

## 11. Asymptotic Properties of U-Statistics

In the asymptotic theory of U-statistics, the main challenge is: when $m > 1$, the U-statistic $U_n$ is **not** a simple sum of independent random variables (even though the original sample $\{X_i\}$ is independent, different subsamples overlap). This prevents direct application of the classical central limit theorem (CLT) for independent random variables.

**Solution**: Find the **projection** of $U_n$ onto the individual observations $X_1, \dots, X_n$.
Specifically, by computing the conditional expectations $E(U_n|X_i)$ ($i=1, \dots, n$), we construct a new statistic:

\[
\tilde{U}_n = EU_n + \sum_{i=1}^n \{E(U_n|X_i) - EU_n\}
\]

Since $X_i$ are independent, $\tilde{U}_n$ is a sum of i.i.d. random variables, and the CLT can be applied directly. If we can show that the residual term $U_n - \tilde{U}_n$ is asymptotically negligible, then by Slutsky's theorem, the asymptotic normality of $U_n$ follows.

### 11.1 Definition and Lemma for Projection of U-statistics

!!! info "Definition 9.6 (Projection)"

    Let $U_n$ be a U-statistic based on the sample $\{X_1, \dots, X_n\}$. The **projection** of $U_n$ onto $\{X_1, \dots, X_n\}$ is defined as:

    \[
    \tilde{U}_n = EU_n + \sum_{i=1}^n \{E(U_n|X_i) - EU_n\} := \theta + \sum_{i=1}^n \{\varphi_n(X_i) - \theta\} \quad \text{--- (9.1)}
    \]

    where $\varphi_n(X_i) = E(U_n|X_i)$.

    If $\{X_i\}$ are i.i.d. (or independent), then the sequence $\{\varphi_n(X_i)\}$ is also i.i.d. (or independent).
    Clearly, by properties of conditional expectation, $E\tilde{U}_n = \theta$ (if $h = h_m$, then equals $EU_n$).

Based on the covariance property $cov(U_n, \tilde{U}_n) = var(\tilde{U}_n)$ (see Shao p179), we obtain the following key lemma regarding the variance of the projection error:

!!! success "Lemma 9.7"

    Let $U_n$ be a U-statistic with $var(U_n) < \infty$ for each $n$. Then:

    \[
    E(U_n - \tilde{U}_n)^2 = var(U_n - \tilde{U}_n) = var(U_n) - var(\tilde{U}_n)
    \]

### 11.2 Asymptotic Distribution Theorem for U-statistics

!!! success "Theorem 9.8 (Limit distribution of U-statistics)"

    Let $U_n$ be a U-statistic based on i.i.d. sample $\{X_i\}_{i=1}^n$, and assume $Eh^2(X_1, \dots, X_m) < \infty$.

    * **(i) Non-degenerate case**: If the first-order projection variance $\xi_1 = var(\tilde{h}_1(X)) > 0$, then:

    \[
    \sqrt{n}(U_n - EU_n) \xrightarrow{d} N(0, m^2\xi_1)
    \]

    * **(ii) First-order degenerate case**: If $\xi_1 = 0$ but $\xi_2 > 0$, then:

    \[
    n(U_n - EU_n) \xrightarrow{d} \frac{m(m-1)}{2} \sum_{j=1}^\infty \lambda_j(\chi_{1,j}^2 - 1)
    \]

    where $\{\chi_{1,j}^2\}_{j \ge 1}$ are i.i.d. $\chi_1^2$ random variables, and $\lambda_j$ are constants (eigenvalues) satisfying $\sum_{j=1}^\infty \lambda_j^2 = \xi_2$.

---

### 11.3 Proof of Theorem 9.8 (i) (Non-degenerate case)

??? proof "Proof of Theorem 9.8 (i) (click to expand)"

    First, compute the conditional expectation $E(U_n|X_1)$:

    \[
    E(U_n|X_1) = \binom{n}{m}^{-1} \sum_{I} E(h(X_{i_1}, \dots, X_{i_m})|X_1)
    \]

    Split it into two parts:

    \[
    = \binom{n}{m}^{-1} \left\{ \sum_{I_1} E(h(X_{i_1}, \dots, X_{i_m})|X_1) + \sum_{I_2} \theta \right\}
    \]

    where $I_1$ consists of all combinations $(i_1, \dots, i_m)$ that contain index $1$, and $I_2$ consists of the other combinations not containing $1$. By combinatorial counting:

    \[
    |I_1| = \binom{n-1}{m-1}, \quad |I_2| = \binom{n-1}{m}
    \]

    Substituting:

    \[
    E(U_n|X_1) = \frac{m!(n-m)!}{n!} \left[ \frac{(n-1)!}{(m-1)!(n-m)!} h_1(X_1) + \frac{(n-1)!}{m!(n-m-1)!} \theta \right]
    \]

    After simplification:

    \[
    = \frac{m}{n} h_1(X_1) + \frac{n-m}{n} \theta
    \]

    Next, construct the projection statistic $\tilde{U}_n$:

    \[
    \tilde{U}_n = \theta + \sum_{i=1}^n \left\{ \frac{m}{n} h_1(X_i) + \frac{n-m}{n} \theta - \theta \right\}
    \]

    Simplify:

    \[
    = \theta + \frac{m}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \{h_1(X_i) - \theta\} = \theta + \frac{m}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \tilde{h}_1(X_i)
    \]

    By the CLT for i.i.d. random variables, since $E\tilde{h}_1^2(X_1) < \infty$:

    \[
    \sqrt{n}(\tilde{U}_n - \theta) \xrightarrow{d} N(0, m^2\xi_1)
    \]

    provided $\xi_1 > 0$ (because $var(\tilde{U}_n) = m^2\xi_1/n$).

    On the other hand, applying Lemma 9.7:

    \[
    E(U_n - \tilde{U}_n)^2 = var(U_n) - var(\tilde{U}_n)
    \]

    Using the expansion of $var(U_n)$ from Theorem 9.5:

    \[
    = \frac{m^2\xi_1}{n} + O(n^{-2}) - \frac{m^2\xi_1}{n} = O(n^{-2})
    \]

    By Chebyshev's inequality, for any $\epsilon > 0$:

    \[
    P(\sqrt{n}|U_n - \tilde{U}_n| > \epsilon) \le \frac{n E(U_n - \tilde{U}_n)^2}{\epsilon^2} = O(n^{-1}) \rightarrow 0
    \]

    thus $\sqrt{n}(U_n - \tilde{U}_n) = o_p(1)$.
    Finally, using the algebraic identity:

    \[
    \sqrt{n}(U_n - \theta) = \sqrt{n}(\tilde{U}_n - \theta) + \sqrt{n}(U_n - \tilde{U}_n)
    \]

    Applying Slutsky's theorem:

    \[
    = \sqrt{n}(\tilde{U}_n - \theta) + o_p(1) \xrightarrow{d} N(0, m^2\xi_1)
    \]

    This completes the proof. $\square$

---

### 11.4 Proof of Theorem 9.8 (ii) (Degenerate case)

Degenerate U-statistics correspond to $\xi_1 = 0$. In this case, the scaling factor becomes $n$ instead of $\sqrt{n}$, and the limiting distribution is a linear combination of infinite chi-square variables.

??? proof "Proof of Theorem 9.8 (ii) (click to expand)"

    We now prove the degenerate U-statistic theorem 9.8(ii). Our goal is to show:

    \[
    n(\hat{U}_n - \theta) := \frac{m(m-1)}{n-1} \sum_{1 \le i < j \le n} \tilde{h}_2(X_i, X_j) \xrightarrow{d} \frac{m(m-1)}{2} Y
    \]

    where $Y = \sum_{j=1}^\infty \lambda_j(W_j^2 - 1)$, and $W_1^2, W_2^2, \dots$ are independent $\chi_1^2$ random variables.
    Let:

    \[
    T_n = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i \ne j} \tilde{h}_2(X_i, X_j)
    \]

    Then:

    \[
    n(\hat{U}_n - \theta) = \frac{m(m-1)}{2} \frac{n}{n-1} T_n
    \]

    Therefore, the goal reduces to proving $T_n \xrightarrow{d} Y$. We will use characteristic functions to prove this, i.e., we need to show for any $x$:

    \[
    E_F\{e^{ixT_n}\} \rightarrow E\{e^{ixY}\}, \quad n \rightarrow \infty \quad \text{--- (9.2)}
    \]

    We will use a special representation of $\tilde{h}_2(x, y)$. Let $\{\phi_j(\cdot)\}$ be orthonormal eigenfunctions associated with eigenvalues $\{\lambda_j\}$ corresponding to $\tilde{h}_2$.
    That is, they satisfy:

    \[
    E_F\{\phi_j(X)\phi_k(X)\} = \begin{cases} 1, & j = k \\ 0, & j \ne k \end{cases}
    \]

    and $\tilde{h}_2(x, y)$ can be expressed as the mean square limit of $\sum_{k=1}^K \lambda_k \phi_k(x) \phi_k(y)$ as $K \rightarrow \infty$. That is:

    \[
    \lim_{K \rightarrow \infty} E_F\left\{ \left[ \tilde{h}_2(X_1, X_2) - \sum_{k=1}^K \lambda_k \phi_k(X_1) \phi_k(X_2) \right]^2 \right\} = 0
    \]

    In the mean square sense we write:

    \[
    \tilde{h}_2(x, y) = \sum_{k=1}^\infty \lambda_k \phi_k(x) \phi_k(y) \quad \text{--- (9.3)}
    \]

    and correspondingly:

    \[
    \tilde{h}_1(x) = \sum_{k=1}^\infty \lambda_k \phi_k(x) E_F\{\phi_k(X)\} \quad \text{--- (9.4)}
    \]

    Since the degeneracy condition $\xi_1 = 0$ holds, we must have for all $k$:

    \[
    E_F\{\phi_k(X)\} = 0 \quad \text{--- (9.5)}
    \]

    Moreover, by orthogonality:

    \[
    E_F\left\{ \left[ \tilde{h}_2(X_1, X_2) - \sum_{k=1}^K \lambda_k \phi_k(X_1) \phi_k(X_2) \right]^2 \right\} = E_F\{\tilde{h}_2^2(X_1, X_2)\} - \sum_{k=1}^K \lambda_k^2
    \]

    which implies that the sum of squares of eigenvalues converges:

    \[
    \sum_{k=1}^\infty \lambda_k^2 = E_F\{\tilde{h}_2^2(X_1, X_2)\} < \infty
    \]

    Substituting the representation into $T_n$ yields:

    \[
    T_n = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i \ne j} \sum_{k=1}^\infty \lambda_k \phi_k(X_i) \phi_k(X_j)
    \]

    Now truncate to the first $K$ terms and define $T_{nK}$:

    \[
    T_{nK} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i \ne j} \sum_{k=1}^K \lambda_k \phi_k(X_i) \phi_k(X_j)
    \]

    Using the inequality $|e^{iz} - 1| \le |z|$ for complex numbers, we can bound the truncation error of the characteristic function:

    \[
    |E\{e^{ixT_n}\} - E\{e^{ixT_{nK}}\}| \le E|e^{ixT_n} - e^{ixT_{nK}}| \le |x| E|T_n - T_{nK}| \le |x| [E(T_n - T_{nK})^2]^{1/2} \quad \text{--- (9.6)}
    \]

    Observe that $T_n - T_{nK}$ is itself a U-statistic:

    \[
    T_n - T_{nK} = \frac{2}{n} \binom{n}{2} U_{nK}
    \]

    where:

    \[
    U_{nK} = \binom{n}{2}^{-1} \sum_{i < j} g_K(X_i, X_j)
    \]

    with the truncated kernel:

    \[
    g_K(x, y) = \tilde{h}_2(x, y) - \sum_{k=1}^K \lambda_k \phi_k(x) \phi_k(y)
    \]

    It can be verified that this truncated kernel satisfies:

    \[
    E_F\{g_K(X_1, X_2)\} = 0
    \]

    \[
    E_F\{g_K^2(X_1, X_2)\} = \sum_{k=K+1}^\infty \lambda_k^2
    \]

    \[
    E_F\{g_K(x, X)\} = 0
    \]

    Hence $E(U_{nK}) = 0$, and by Theorem 9.5:

    \[
    E\{U_{nK}^2\} = \binom{n}{2}^{-1} \sum_{k=K+1}^\infty \lambda_k^2
    \]

    Therefore:

    \[
    E(T_n - T_{nK})^2 = (n-1)^2 \binom{n}{2}^{-1} \sum_{k=K+1}^\infty \lambda_k^2 \le 2 \sum_{k=K+1}^\infty \lambda_k^2 \quad \text{--- (9.7)}
    \]

    Now fix $x$ and let $\epsilon > 0$. Choose and fix $K$ large enough so that:

    \[
    |x| \left( 2 \sum_{k=K+1}^\infty \lambda_k^2 \right)^{1/2} < \epsilon
    \]

    Combining (9.6) and (9.7), we have established:

    \[
    |E\{e^{ixT_n}\} - E\{e^{ixT_{nK}}\}| < \epsilon, \quad \text{for all } n \quad \text{--- (9.8)}
    \]

    Next we prove:

    \[
    T_{nK} \Rightarrow Y_K = \sum_{k=1}^K \lambda_k (W_k^2 - 1) \quad \text{--- (9.9)}
    \]

    We can write $T_{nK}$ as:

    \[
    T_{nK} = \sum_{k=1}^K \lambda_k (W_{kn}^2 - Z_{kn})
    \]

    where $W_{kn} = n^{-1/2} \sum_{i=1}^n \phi_k(X_i)$ and $Z_{kn} = n^{-1} \sum_{i=1}^n \phi_k^2(X_i)$.
    From the earlier conditions, for all $k$ and $n$, $E(W_{kn}) = 0$ and:

    \[
    Cov\{W_{jn}, W_{kn}\} = \begin{cases} 1, & j = k \\ 0, & j \ne k \end{cases} \quad \text{for all } j, k \text{ and } n
    \]

    Thus, by the multivariate Lindeberg-Levy CLT:

    \[
    (W_{1n}, \dots, W_{Kn}) \xrightarrow{d} N(0, I_{K \times K})
    \]

    Meanwhile, because $E_F\{\phi_k^2(X)\} = 1$, the classical strong law of large numbers (SLLN) gives:

    \[
    (Z_{1n}, \dots, Z_{Kn}) \xrightarrow{wp1} (1, \dots, 1)
    \]

    This proves (9.9), i.e., for sufficiently large $n$:

    \[
    |E\{e^{ixT_{nK}}\} - E\{e^{ixY_K}\}| < 2\epsilon \quad \text{--- (9.10)}
    \]

    Finally, we prove:

    \[
    |E\{e^{ixY_K}\} - E\{e^{ixY}\}| < \epsilon [E(W_1^2 - 1)^2]^{1/2}, \quad \text{for all } n \quad \text{--- (9.11)}
    \]

    To complete this step, we define the random variables $W_1^2, W_2^2, \dots$ on a common probability space, and express $Y$ as the mean square limit of $Y_K$ as $K \rightarrow \infty$. Then:

    \[
    |E\{e^{ixY_K}\} - E\{e^{ixY}\}| \le |x| [E(Y - Y_K)^2]^{1/2}
    \]

    \[
    \le |x| [E(W_1^2 - 1)^2]^{1/2} \left[ \sum_{k=K+1}^\infty \lambda_k^2 \right]^{1/2}
    \]

    This yields (9.11). Combining (9.8), (9.10), and (9.11), for any $x$, any $\epsilon > 0$, and sufficiently large $n$, we have:

    \[
    |E\{e^{ixT_n}\} - E\{e^{ixY}\}| \le \epsilon \{2 + [E(W_1^2 - 1)^2]^{1/2}\}
    \]

    This proves $T_n \xrightarrow{d} Y$ as $n \rightarrow \infty$, which is the desired result of Theorem 9.8 (ii). $\square$

## 12. Degeneracy and Hypothesis Testing

Many test statistics in practical applications have the following pattern:

* **Non-degenerate under the alternative hypothesis $H_1$**;

* **Degenerate under the null hypothesis $H_0$**.

**Classic examples:**

* **MMD (Maximum Mean Discrepancy)**: When $P \ne Q$, it has a normal limit with $\sqrt{n}$ rate; under $P = Q$, it has a degenerate weighted chi-squared limiting distribution.

* **HSIC (Hilbert-Schmidt Independence Criterion)**: When variables are dependent, it has a normal limit with $\sqrt{n}$ rate; when variables are independent, it exhibits a degenerate limit.

* **Cramer-von Mises test and integrated squared density test**: Their null hypothesis limits typically come from degenerate quadratic forms.

**Inference consequence:**

The normal approximation that holds under a fixed alternative **typically fails under the null hypothesis**.
Therefore, when conducting hypothesis tests, we often need to resort to **Permutation**, **Spectral** or **Bootstrap calibration** to obtain correct critical values.

## 13. Classical Application Examples of U-statistics

### 13.1 Example 1: Sample Variance

!!! example "Example 1: Sample variance"

    Let the population satisfy $\mu = E X$ and $\sigma^2 = Var(X)$, and assume the fourth central moment is finite $\mu_4 = E(X-\mu)^4 < \infty$.
  
    Use the kernel:

    \[
    h(x, y) = \frac{(x-y)^2}{2}
    \]

    The target parameter to be estimated is $\theta = \sigma^2$.

    Compute its first projection function $h_1(x)$:

    \[
    h_1(x) = E \left\{ \frac{(x-X')^2}{2} \right\} = \frac{(x-\mu)^2 + \sigma^2}{2}
    \]

    Thus we obtain the centered first projection:

    \[
    h_1(x) - \theta = \frac{(x-\mu)^2 - \sigma^2}{2}
    \]

    Compute the variance of the first projection $\xi_1$:

    \[
    \xi_1 = \frac{1}{4} Var\{(X-\mu)^2\} = \frac{\mu_4 - \sigma^4}{4}
    \]

    The corresponding U-statistic is exactly the unbiased sample variance:

    \[
    U_n = \binom{n}{2}^{-1} \sum_{i<j} \frac{(X_i - X_j)^2}{2} = \frac{1}{n-1} \sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar{X}_n)^2
    \]

    If $\mu_4 > \sigma^4$, this is the non-degenerate case, and by the Central Limit Theorem (CLT) we have:

    \[
    \sqrt{n}(U_n - \sigma^2) \xrightarrow{d} N(0, 4\xi_1) = N(0, \mu_4 - \sigma^4)
    \]

    **Note**: For a Gaussian (normal) data distribution, since $\mu_4 = 3\sigma^4$, the asymptotic variance is $2\sigma^4$.

### 13.2 Example 2: Product Kernel and Degenerate Boundary

!!! example "Example 2: Product kernel"

    Let the kernel be $h(x, y) = x y$. Then:

    \[
    \theta = (E X)^2 = \mu^2
    \]

    The first projection function and its variance are:

    \[
    h_1(x) = \mu x, \quad \xi_1 = Var\{\mu X\} = \mu^2 \sigma^2
    \]

    **Case 1: Non-degenerate limit**

    * If $\mu \ne 0$, the statistic is non-degenerate:

    \[
    \sqrt{n}(U_n - \mu^2) \xrightarrow{d} N(0, 4\mu^2 \sigma^2)
    \]

    **Case 2: Degenerate limit**

    * If $\mu = 0$, the variance of the first projection $\xi_1$ vanishes, and the statistic becomes **degenerate**.
  
    This clearly shows that whether a U-statistic is degenerate may depend on the underlying true data distribution $P$.

    In the degenerate case $\mu = 0$, we can explicitly write its limiting distribution:

    \[
    U_n = \frac{1}{n(n-1)} \sum_{i \ne j} X_i X_j
    \]

    Since $\bar{X}_n^2 = \frac{1}{n^2} \left\{ \sum_{i \ne j} X_i X_j + \sum_{i=1}^n X_i^2 \right\}$, we obtain:

    \[
    n U_n = \frac{n}{n-1} n \bar{X}_n^2 - \frac{1}{n-1} \sum_{i=1}^n X_i^2
    \]

    By the Central Limit Theorem and the Law of Large Numbers:

    \[
    n U_n \xrightarrow{d} \sigma^2(\chi_1^2 - 1)
    \]

    This perfectly matches the limit result from second-order degenerate theory containing one non-zero eigenvalue.

### 13.3 Example 3: Gini Mean Difference

!!! example "Example 3: Gini mean difference"

    For a univariate distribution, the target parameter of the Gini mean difference is:

    \[
    \theta = E|X_1 - X_2|
    \]

    Its U-statistic estimator is:

    \[
    U_n = \binom{n}{2}^{-1} \sum_{i<j} |X_i - X_j|
    \]

    The first projection function is:

    \[
    h_1(x) = E|x - X'|
    \]

    If $Var\{h_1(X)\} > 0$, then:

    \[
    \sqrt{n}(U_n - \theta) \xrightarrow{d} N(0, 4 Var\{h_1(X)\})
    \]

    This is an example of a robust measure of scale/dispersion, and differentiability of the kernel is not required in this process.

### 13.4 Example 4: Kendall's tau

!!! example "Example 4: Kendall's tau"

    For bivariate data $Z = (X, Y)$, define the kernel:

    \[
    h(z_1, z_2) = \text{sign}\{(x_1 - x_2)(y_1 - y_2)\}
    \]

    Its U-statistic estimator is:

    \[
    \hat{\tau} = \binom{n}{2}^{-1} \sum_{i<j} h(Z_i, Z_j)
    \]

    This statistic estimates:

    \[
    \tau = \mathbb{P}\{(X_1 - X_2)(Y_1 - Y_2) > 0\} - \mathbb{P}\{(X_1 - X_2)(Y_1 - Y_2) < 0\}
    \]

    * It is a nonparametric measure of dependence.

    * In the non-degenerate case, its asymptotic variance is determined by $4 Var\{h_1(Z)\}$.

    * Rank tests are one of the earliest and most classical origins of U-statistic theory.

## 14. Asymptotic Confidence Intervals and Hypothesis Testing

### 14.1 Constructing Asymptotic Confidence Intervals

In the non-degenerate case, we know:

\[
\sqrt{n}(U_n - \theta) \xrightarrow{d} N(0, m^2\xi_1)
\]

Based on this, we can construct a natural confidence interval:

\[
U_n \pm z_{1-\alpha/2} \frac{m\hat{\sigma}_1}{\sqrt{n}}
\]

where $\hat{\sigma}_1^2$ is a consistent estimator of the projection variance $\xi_1 = Var\{h_1(X)\}$.

The difficulty is that the function $h_1(x) = E h(x, X_2, \dots, X_m)$ depends on the unknown population distribution $P$.

### 14.2 Estimating the Variance of the First Projection

To estimate $\xi_1$, we define a **leave-one-in average** for each observation $i$:

\[
\hat{h}_{1,i} = \binom{n-1}{m-1}^{-1} \sum_{i \in A, |A|=m} h(X_A)
\]

This is the average of the kernel over all possible subsamples that include the observation $X_i$. It is effectively an estimator of $h_1(X_i)$.

Then we compute the sample variance of these values:

\[
\hat{\xi}_1 = \frac{1}{n-1} \sum_{i=1}^n (\hat{h}_{1,i} - U_n)^2
\]

Under standard regularity conditions with fixed $m$, it can be shown that:

\[
\hat{\xi}_1 \xrightarrow{p} \xi_1
\]

Therefore, the standard error estimate of the U-statistic is:

\[
\widehat{se}(U_n) = \frac{m\sqrt{\hat{\xi}_1}}{\sqrt{n}}
\]

### 14.3 Standard Template for Hypothesis Testing

Suppose we wish to test $H_0: \theta = \theta_0$, and we know that the statistic is **non-degenerate** under the null hypothesis.

We can construct the following standardized test statistic:

\[
Z_n = \frac{\sqrt{n}(U_n - \theta_0)}{m\sqrt{\hat{\xi}_1}} \xrightarrow{d} N(0, 1)
\]

When $|Z_n| > z_{1-\alpha/2}$, we reject the null hypothesis at significance level $\alpha$.

!!! warning "Degeneracy Warning"

    If $\xi_1 = 0$ under the null hypothesis $H_0$, the construction of the above statistic is **invalid**. The correct scaling and null limiting distribution will be completely different.
  
    This warning is an extremely important core issue in kernel two-sample tests and kernel independence tests.

## 15. Two-Sample and Higher-Order U-statistics

### 15.1 Two-Sample U-statistics

Many statistics are used to compare two independent samples:

\[
X_1, \dots, X_n \stackrel{iid}{\sim} P, \quad Y_1, \dots, Y_N \stackrel{iid}{\sim} Q
\]

The general form of a two-sample U-statistic is:

\[
U_{n,N} = \binom{n}{r}^{-1} \binom{N}{s}^{-1} \sum_{I, J} h(X_I, Y_J)
\]

Classic examples include:

* Mann-Whitney / Wilcoxon statistic;

* Unbiased MMD estimator;

* Energy distance statistics.

The same projection philosophy applies to the two-sample case, with the difference that conditional projections onto the $X$ sample and the $Y$ sample need to be computed separately.

### 15.2 AUC and Ranking Loss

Let $S$ be the score given by a model, and $P_+$ and $P_-$ be the distributions of the positive and negative classes, respectively. AUC is defined as:

\[
AUC = \mathbb{P}(S_+ > S_-) + \frac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}(S_+ = S_-)
\]

The empirical AUC estimator is:

\[
\widehat{AUC} = \frac{1}{n_+ n_-} \sum_{i=1}^{n_+} \sum_{j=1}^{n_-} \left\{ \mathbb{I}(S_i^+ > S_j^-) + \frac{1}{2}\mathbb{I}(S_i^+ = S_j^-) \right\}
\]

This is clearly a two-sample U-statistic. Its central limit theorem can be used to compute standard errors for classifier comparisons, which explains why pairwise ranking losses are naturally closely related to U-statistic theory.

### 15.3 MMD: Degeneracy under the Null Hypothesis

For MMD, its second-order kernel is:

\[
h(Z_i, Z_j) = k(X_i, X_j) + k(Y_i, Y_j) - k(X_i, Y_j) - k(X_j, Y_i)
\]

Under the null hypothesis $H_0: P = Q$:

\[
E\{h(Z_i, Z_j) | Z_i\} = 0
\]

This implies $\xi_1 = 0$. Therefore, it has a weighted chi-squared limit:

\[
n \widehat{MMD}_u^2 \xrightarrow{d} \sum_{j=1}^\infty \lambda_j (Z_j^2 - 1)
\]

The eigenvalues depend on the centered kernel. Under a fixed alternative $H_1: P \ne Q$, the first projection is typically non-zero, and then:

\[
\sqrt{n}(\widehat{MMD}_u^2 - MMD^2(P,Q))
\]

will follow an asymptotic normal distribution.

### 15.4 HSIC: A Higher-Order Example

The unbiased HSIC estimator can be written as a **fourth-order U-statistic**:

\[
\widehat{HSIC}_u = \binom{n}{4}^{-1} \sum_{1 \le i < j < q < r \le n} h(Z_i, Z_j, Z_q, Z_r)
\]

Its symmetric kernel is constructed from the product of centered $X$ and $Y$ kernels.

* If $X$ and $Y$ are **dependent**, the first projection is non-zero, and the statistic has a $\sqrt{n}$-rate normal limit.

* If $X \perp Y$ (independent), the first projection vanishes, and the null limit is **degenerate**.

* Since independence implies exchangeability under the null hypothesis, **permutation calibration** is a very natural and effective approach.

## 16. Incomplete and Parameter-Dependent U-statistics in Scalable Learning

### 16.1 Incomplete U-statistics for Scalable Learning

A full U-statistic requires enumeration of all $\binom{n}{m}$ subsets, which can be computationally prohibitive for large sample sizes.

**Incomplete U-statistics** randomly draw $B$ subsets $I_1, \dots, I_B$ and define:

\[
U_{n,B}^{inc} = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{b=1}^B h(X_{I_b})
\]

* If the subsets are sampled uniformly, it remains unbiased conditional on the data.

* Its variance consists of two parts: **sampling noise** from the data itself and **Monte Carlo noise** from subset sampling.

* In modern machine learning, this approach is widely used in large-scale kernel tests, Bagging, and the optimization of pairwise/ranking objective functions.

### 16.2 Integrated Squared Error Statistic

In nonparametric density estimation, let the kernel density estimator (KDE) be:

\[
\hat{f}_n(x) = \frac{1}{n b_n} \sum_{i=1}^n K\left( \frac{x - X_i}{b_n} \right) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n K_{b_n}(x - X_i)
\]

where $K_b(t) = b^{-1} K(t/b)$, satisfying $b_n \rightarrow 0$, $n b_n \rightarrow \infty$.
If we wish to test goodness-of-fit $H_0: f = f_{\theta}$, a natural statistic is:

\[
T_n = \int \{ \hat{f}_n(x) - f_{\hat{\theta}_n}(x) \}^2 dx
\]

The random quadratic part of this statistic can be expressed as a U-statistic whose kernel depends on the sample size $n$.

### 16.3 The n-dependent Degenerate Kernel

Let:

\[
\mu_n(y) = E K_{b_n}(y - X)
\]

The dominant random quadratic term involves the following kernel:

\[
h_n(x_1, x_2) = \frac{n-1}{n} \int \{K_{b_n}(y - x_1) - \mu_n(y)\} \{K_{b_n}(y - x_2) - \mu_n(y)\} dy
\]

In this case:

\[
E\{h_n(x, X_2)\} = 0
\]

Therefore, the dominant off-diagonal term is a **second-order degenerate U-statistic with an n-dependent kernel**.

**Conclusion insight:** Smooth goodness-of-fit tests typically reduce to studying degenerate quadratic U-statistics, but the kernel and variance change with the smoothing bandwidth $b_n$.

## 17. Summary and Practical Guide

### 17.1 How to Analyze a New U-statistic?

If you encounter a new U-statistic in your research, you can follow these steps:

* **Identify the kernel** $h$ and the target parameter $\theta = E h(X_1, \dots, X_m)$ to be estimated.

* If the initial kernel is not symmetric, **symmetrize it**.

* Compute the **first projection** $h_1(x)$.

* Check whether the first projection variance $\xi_1 = Var\{h_1(X)\}$ is **positive** (especially under the null hypothesis $H_0$ for hypothesis testing).

* **If $\xi_1 > 0$**: Use the standard central limit theorem with $\sqrt{n}$ rate, asymptotic variance $m^2\xi_1$.

* **If $\xi_1 = 0$**: Compute the first non-zero higher-order projection. Expect a faster convergence rate and a non-Gaussian limiting distribution.

* For practical inference, either estimate the projection variance via a consistent estimator, or use resampling/spectral methods appropriate under the null hypothesis.

### 17.2 Main Takeaways

* **Essential construction**: U-statistics arise very naturally as Rao-Blackwellizations of naive unbiased estimators from unordered samples.

* **Unbiased complete averaging**: They estimate expectations of functionals involving multiple observations by complete symmetric averaging over all combinations.

* **Handling dependence**: Although summands are dependent, this can be rigorously handled via overlap counting and conditional projection arguments.

* **Hoeffding decomposition**: This decomposition technique neatly separates the statistic into orthogonal interaction terms of different orders.

* **Non-degenerate case**: Non-degenerate U-statistics satisfy asymptotic normality with variance $m^2\xi_1/n$.

* **Degenerate case**: Degenerate U-statistics have completely different convergence rates, and the limiting distribution is typically a weighted chi-squared or higher-order chaos limit.

* **Practical relevance**: Degeneracy is far from a theoretical "pathological case"; it is absolutely central in modern kernel testing.
