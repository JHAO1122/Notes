## Problem 1

Let $(X, \mathcal{T})$ be a Hausdorff space. Let $A, B \subseteq X$ be two disjoint compact subsets.

Prove that there exist disjoint open sets $U, V$ in $X$ such that $A \subseteq U$ and $B \subseteq V$.

??? success "Solution"

    **Proof:**

    First fix an arbitrary point $a \in A$.

    For every point $b \in B$, since $A \cap B = \emptyset$, we necessarily have $a \ne b$.

    Since $X$ is Hausdorff, for distinct points $a$ and $b$, there exist disjoint open sets $U_{a,b}$ and $V_{a,b}$ in $X$ such that $a \in U_{a,b}$ and $b \in V_{a,b}$.

    As $b$ ranges over the whole set $B$, the family of open sets $\{V_{a,b}\}_{b \in B}$ forms an open cover of the compact set $B$.

    By compactness, there is a finite subcover; that is, there exist finitely many points $b_1, b_2, \dots, b_n \in B$ such that:

    \[
    B \subseteq \bigcup_{i=1}^n V_{a,b_i}
    \]

    For these finitely many points, define:

    \[
    U_a = \bigcap_{i=1}^n U_{a,b_i} \quad \text{and} \quad V_a = \bigcup_{i=1}^n V_{a,b_i}
    \]

    Clearly $U_a$ is a finite intersection of open sets, hence open; $V_a$ is a union of open sets, hence also open. Moreover, since $U_{a,b_i} \cap V_{a,b_i} = \emptyset$ for all $i$, we deduce $U_a \cap V_a = \emptyset$. At this point, $a \in U_a$ and $B \subseteq V_a$.

    Now let $a$ range over the whole set $A$. The family of open sets $\{U_a\}_{a \in A}$ forms an open cover of the compact set $A$.

    Similarly, there exist finitely many points $a_1, a_2, \dots, a_m \in A$ such that the finite subcover holds:

    \[
    A \subseteq \bigcup_{j=1}^m U_{a_j}
    \]

    Finally, define:

    \[
    U = \bigcup_{j=1}^m U_{a_j} \quad \text{and} \quad V = \bigcap_{j=1}^m V_{a_j}
    \]

    Clearly $U$ and $V$ are open sets and satisfy $A \subseteq U$ and $B \subseteq V$.

    Check that they are disjoint:

    \[
    U \cap V = \left( \bigcup_{j=1}^m U_{a_j} \right) \cap V = \bigcup_{j=1}^m (U_{a_j} \cap V) \subseteq \bigcup_{j=1}^m (U_{a_j} \cap V_{a_j}) = \emptyset
    \]

    Therefore, we have found disjoint open sets $U$ and $V$ satisfying the required conditions.

## Problem 2

Prove that a connected metric space $(X, d)$ containing more than one point must be uncountable.

(Hint: Pick $a \in X$ and consider the function $d_a: X \to \mathbb{R}$, defined by $d_a(x) := d(a, x)$. Think about the connectedness of countable subsets of $\mathbb{R}$.)

??? success "Solution"

    **Proof:**

    Since the space $X$ contains more than one point, there exist two points $a, b \in X$ such that $a \ne b$. By positivity of the metric, we have:

    \[
    d(a, b) > 0
    \]

    Fix the point $a$ and define the distance function $d_a: X \to \mathbb{R}$ by:

    \[
    d_a(x) = d(a, x)
    \]

    By the triangle inequality, for any $x, y \in X$, we have $|d_a(x) - d_a(y)| = |d(a, x) - d(a, y)| \le d(x, y)$. Therefore $d_a$ is (uniformly) continuous on $X$.

    We know the following important property of topological spaces: the image of a connected space under a continuous map is connected. Since $X$ is connected, the image $d_a(X)$ must be a connected subset of the real line $\mathbb{R}$.

    Examine the elements of $d_a(X)$. Since $a \in X$ and $b \in X$, the image must contain:

    \[
    d_a(a) = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad d_a(b) = d(a, b) > 0
    \]

    In $\mathbb{R}$, a connected subset containing both $0$ and $d(a,b)$ must be an interval containing the entire closed interval $[0, d(a,b)]$.

    Any real interval of positive length is uncountable. Hence the image $d_a(X)$ is uncountable.

    Since the image of a function is uncountable, its domain $X$ must also be uncountable.

## Problem 3

(A special case of the Baire category theorem) Let $(X, \mathcal{T})$ be a compact Hausdorff space. Let $\{A_i : i \in \mathbb{N}\}$ be a family of closed subsets of $X$, and suppose that for each $i$, its interior is empty, i.e. $(A_i)^\circ = \emptyset$.

Prove that the interior of the union is empty, i.e. $\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^\infty A_i \right)^\circ = \emptyset$.

??? success "Solution"

    **Proof:**

    We argue by contradiction. Suppose the interior of the union is nonempty. Then there exists a nonempty open set $V_0$ such that:

    \[
    V_0 \subseteq \bigcup_{i=1}^\infty A_i
    \]

    Consider the set $A_1$. Since $A_1$ is closed and has empty interior (that is, it contains no nonempty open set), the open set $V_0$ can never be completely contained in $A_1$. Therefore, the set $V_0 \setminus A_1$ is a **nonempty open set**.

    A compact Hausdorff space is necessarily a regular space. In a regular space, every nonempty open set contains the closure of some nonempty open set. Hence there exists a nonempty open set $V_1$ satisfying:

    \[
    \overline{V_1} \subseteq V_0 \setminus A_1
    \]

    Next consider $A_2$. Similarly, since $A_2$ has empty interior, $V_1$ cannot be completely contained in $A_2$, so $V_1 \setminus A_2$ is also nonempty open. By regularity, we can find a nonempty open set $V_2$ satisfying:

    \[
    \overline{V_2} \subseteq V_1 \setminus A_2 \subseteq \overline{V_1}
    \]

    Repeating this process indefinitely, we construct a sequence of open sets $\{V_n\}$ such that for every $k \ge 1$:

    \[
    \overline{V_k} \subseteq V_{k-1} \setminus A_k \subseteq \overline{V_{k-1}}
    \]

    Thus we obtain a nested decreasing sequence of nonempty closed sets:

    \[
    \overline{V_1} \supseteq \overline{V_2} \supseteq \overline{V_3} \supseteq \dots
    \]

    Since $X$ is compact and this family of closed sets has the finite intersection property, the intersection of the entire decreasing sequence must be nonempty:

    \[
    \bigcap_{k=1}^\infty \overline{V_k} \ne \emptyset
    \]

    Let $x \in \bigcap_{k=1}^\infty \overline{V_k}$. Since all the $\overline{V_k}$ are contained in $V_0$, we have $x \in V_0$. By the initial assumption $V_0 \subseteq \bigcup A_i$, there exists some integer $m$ such that $x \in A_m$.

    However, by the construction of the sequence, $x \in \overline{V_m}$. And by the recursive construction:

    \[
    \overline{V_m} \subseteq V_{m-1} \setminus A_m
    \]

    This implies $x \notin A_m$, which contradicts the conclusion in step 8 that $x \in A_m$.

    This contradiction shows that the initial assumption was false. Therefore the union cannot contain any nonempty open set, i.e. $\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^\infty A_i \right)^\circ = \emptyset$.

## Problem 4

(The Cantor set) Let $A_0 = [0, 1]$, and for each $n \in \mathbb{N}$, define recursively:

\[
A_n = A_{n-1} \setminus \bigcup_{k=0}^{3^{n-1}-1} \left( \frac{1+3k}{3^n}, \frac{2+3k}{3^n} \right)
\]

Let $C := \bigcap_{n \ge 0} A_n$, endowed with the standard subspace topology inherited from $\mathbb{R}$.

Prove the following:
(i) $C$ is totally disconnected (i.e. every connected component of $C$ is a singleton).
(ii) $C$ is compact and has no isolated points, and deduce that $C$ is uncountable.

??? success "Solution"

    **(i) Prove that $C$ is totally disconnected**

    Suppose, to the contrary, that $C$ has a connected component $S$ containing more than one point.

    Take two points $x, y \in S$ with $x < y$. Since $x, y \in S \subseteq C$, we must have $x, y \in A_n$ for all $n \ge 0$.

    Choose $n$ large enough so that the length of the removed intervals satisfies:

    \[
    \frac{1}{3^n} < y - x
    \]

    At the $n$-th stage of the construction, $A_n$ consists of a union of disjoint closed intervals of length $\frac{1}{3^n}$.

    Since $y - x > 1/3^n$, the points $x$ and $y$ cannot lie in the same closed interval of $A_n$.

    Therefore, between $x$ and $y$ there must be at least one open interval that was removed during the construction (e.g. the middle third). Let $z$ be a point in one such removed interval. Then $x < z < y$ and $z \notin C$.

    But a connected subset $S$ of the real line has the property that if $x, y \in S$, then the entire interval $[x, y]$ must be contained in $S$. This contradicts the fact that $z \notin C$. Therefore every connected component of $C$ is a singleton.

    **(ii) Compactness, absence of isolated points, and uncountability**

    **Compactness:** Each $A_n$ is a finite union of closed intervals, hence closed in $[0, 1]$. The intersection $C = \bigcap A_n$ of arbitrarily many closed sets is still closed in $[0, 1]$. Since $[0, 1]$ is a closed and bounded subset of $\mathbb{R}$ (hence compact), its closed subset $C$ is necessarily **compact**.

    **No isolated points:** Take any $x \in C$ and any relatively open neighbourhood $V$ of $x$ in $C$. There exists an open set $U$ in $\mathbb{R}$ such that $V = U \cap C$. Since $U$ is open, there exists $\epsilon > 0$ such that $(x - \epsilon, x + \epsilon) \subseteq U$.

    For every $n$, $x \in A_n$, so $x$ lies in one of the closed intervals $I_n$ of $A_n$; this interval has length $1/3^n$.

    By construction, the two endpoints of $I_n$ are never removed at later stages, so they both belong to $C$.

    Choose $n$ large enough so that $1/3^n < \epsilon$. Then the whole interval $I_n$ is contained in $(x - \epsilon, x + \epsilon)$.

    The two endpoints of $I_n$ belong to $V$. Even if $x$ happens to be one of these endpoints, the other endpoint is distinct from $x$ and belongs to $V$. This shows that every neighbourhood of $x$ contains a point of $C$ different from $x$. Hence $x$ is not an isolated point (i.e. $C$ is perfect).

    **Uncountability:** We have shown that $C$ is compact (hence a complete metric space) with no isolated points. By the Baire category theorem, since a countable union of nowhere dense singleton sets cannot constitute a complete space, $C$ must be **uncountable**.

## Problem 5

Fix a compact topological space $(X, \mathcal{T})$. Prove that the following two statements are equivalent:

(i) With respect to $\mathcal{T}$, every compact subset of $X$ is closed in $X$;
(ii) For any topology $\mathcal{T}'$ strictly finer than $\mathcal{T}$, the space $(X, \mathcal{T}')$ is non-compact.

Also, give an example of a space $(X, \mathcal{T})$ satisfying the above (equivalent) conditions.

??? success "Solution"

    **Proof of (i) $\implies$ (ii)**

    Let $\mathcal{T}'$ be a topology strictly finer than $\mathcal{T}$ (i.e. $\mathcal{T}' \supsetneq \mathcal{T}$).

    Because the topology is finer, there exists an open set $U \in \mathcal{T}' \setminus \mathcal{T}$.

    Let $K = X \setminus U$. Clearly $K$ is closed in $\mathcal{T}'$, but not closed in $\mathcal{T}$.

    We argue by contradiction. Suppose the space $(X, \mathcal{T}')$ is still compact.

    Since $K$ is a closed subset of the compact space $(X, \mathcal{T}')$, by standard topological properties $K$ is compact in $(X, \mathcal{T}')$.

    Consider the identity map $id: (X, \mathcal{T}') \to (X, \mathcal{T})$. Since $\mathcal{T}'$ contains $\mathcal{T}$, this map is continuous.

    The continuous image of a compact set is compact. Therefore $K$ must also be compact in $(X, \mathcal{T})$.

    By assumption (i), every compact subset of $(X, \mathcal{T})$ must be closed. Thus $K$ must be closed in $\mathcal{T}$.

    This contradicts the conclusion in step 3 that $K$ is not closed in $\mathcal{T}$. Therefore $(X, \mathcal{T}')$ cannot be compact.

    **Proof of (ii) $\implies$ (i)**

    Again we argue by contradiction. Suppose condition (i) is false; that is, there exists a compact subset $K$ of $X$ which is not closed in $\mathcal{T}$.

    Since $K$ is not closed, its complement $X \setminus K$ is not open in $\mathcal{T}$.

    We construct a new topology $\mathcal{T}'$ by adding $X \setminus K$ to the family of open sets. Let its subbasis be $\mathcal{T} \cup \{X \setminus K\}$. Since $X \setminus K \notin \mathcal{T}$, the new topology $\mathcal{T}'$ is strictly finer than $\mathcal{T}$.

    We now show that under this finer topology, $(X, \mathcal{T}')$ is still compact.

    Let $\{U_\alpha\} \cup \{V_\beta \setminus K\}$ be an arbitrary open cover of $X$ in the new topology $\mathcal{T}'$, where $U_\alpha, V_\beta \in \mathcal{T}$.

    Since sets of the form $V_\beta \setminus K$ cannot cover any point of $K$, the subset $K$ must be entirely covered by the original open sets $\{U_\alpha\}$.

    Since $K$ is compact in $\mathcal{T}$, there exists a finite subcover $\{U_{\alpha_1}, \dots, U_{\alpha_n}\}$ covering $K$.

    Let $W = \bigcup_{i=1}^n U_{\alpha_i}$. Then $W$ is a $\mathcal{T}$-open set containing $K$.

    Consider the remaining uncovered part $X \setminus W$. It is $\mathcal{T}$-closed and contained in the compact space $X$. Hence $X \setminus W$ is $\mathcal{T}$-compact.

    Also note that $X \setminus W \subseteq X \setminus K$, so it is covered by the remaining sets in the cover. Using the finite subcover property, only finitely many of these sets are needed to cover $X \setminus W$.

    Hence we have extracted a finite subcover of the whole space $X$, proving that $(X, \mathcal{T}')$ is compact.

    But this contradicts condition (ii), which says every strictly finer topology must be non-compact. Therefore the assumption is false and condition (i) must hold.

    **Example of a space satisfying the conditions**

    Any **compact Hausdorff space** satisfies these conditions.

    A concrete example: the closed interval $[0, 1]$ with the standard topology.

    Reason: in a Hausdorff space, every compact subset is necessarily closed, so it satisfies condition (i) and therefore equivalently condition (ii).

## Problem 6

Determine whether the following two spaces are locally compact:

(i) The rational numbers $\mathbb{Q}$ with the standard topology;
(ii) The real line $(\mathbb{R}, \mathcal{T})$ with the topology generated by the basis $\mathcal{B} = \{(a, b] : a, b \in \mathbb{R}, a < b\}$.

Justify your answers.

??? success "Solution"

    **(i) $\mathbb{Q}$ with the standard topology is not locally compact**

    **Proof:**
    We argue by contradiction. Suppose $\mathbb{Q}$ is locally compact. Then for any point $x \in \mathbb{Q}$, there must exist a compact neighbourhood $K$ of $x$ in $\mathbb{Q}$.

    Since $K$ is a neighbourhood of $x$, there exists $\epsilon > 0$ such that the open set $U = (x - \epsilon, x + \epsilon) \cap \mathbb{Q}$ satisfies:

    \[
    x \in U \subseteq K
    \]

    Since $\mathbb{Q}$ is a metric space, its compact subset $K$ must be **sequentially compact**.

    Now choose an irrational number $y$ in the interval $(x - \epsilon, x + \epsilon)$ (such irrationals exist because they are dense in $\mathbb{R}$).
    By the density of the rationals, we can construct a sequence of rationals $\{x_n\} \subset U \subseteq K$ such that $x_n \to y$ in the usual metric on $\mathbb{R}$.

    Since $K$ is sequentially compact, the sequence $\{x_n\}$ must have a convergent subsequence within $K$ (hence within $\mathbb{Q}$).
    However, in $\mathbb{R}$ the sequence converges uniquely to the irrational number $y \notin \mathbb{Q}$, so in $\mathbb{Q}$ the sequence has no limit point. This is a fundamental contradiction to the sequential compactness of $K$.
    Hence $\mathbb{Q}$ is not locally compact.

    **(ii) $(\mathbb{R}, \mathcal{T})$ with the upper limit topology is not locally compact**

    **Proof:**
    The basic open sets of this topology are left-open right-closed intervals $(a, b]$. We again argue by contradiction.
    Suppose this space is locally compact. Then any point $x \in \mathbb{R}$ must have a compact neighbourhood $K$ containing $x$.

    By definition of neighbourhood, there exists a basis element $(a, x]$ such that:

    \[
    x \in (a, x] \subseteq K
    \]

    Notice that in the upper limit topology, the set $(a, x]$ is both open and closed (its complement $(-\infty, a] \cup (x, \infty)$ can be written as a union of basis elements and is open).
    A closed subset of a compact set $K$ must also be compact, so $(a, x]$ must be compact, and thus must be **limit point compact**.

    We construct a sequence in $(a, x]$ that decreases strictly to $a$, for example:

    \[
    x_n = a + \frac{x - a}{n} \quad (n \ge 1)
    \]

    Clearly $x_n \in (a, x]$ for all $n$.
    For any point $y \in (a, x]$, we may choose a neighbourhood of the form $(c, y]$ in the upper limit topology.
    Since the sequence $x_n$ decreases strictly to $a < y$, for sufficiently large $n$ we have $x_n < c < y$, meaning that this neighbourhood $(c, y]$ contains only finitely many points of the sequence.

    Therefore this sequence has no limit point in $(a, x]$. This contradicts the fact that $(a, x]$, being compact, must be limit point compact.
    Hence this space is not locally compact.

## Problem 7

Prove that the product space $\left(\prod_{i=1}^{\infty} A_i, \mathcal{T}_p\right)$ is compact in the product topology, where each $A_i$ is a nonempty finite set with the discrete topology.

(Hint: Review the conclusion from Homework 5; consider sequential compactness and metrizability.)

??? success "Solution"

    **Proof:**

    First, since each $A_i$ is finite and discrete, it is a metrizable space.
    The product of countably many metrizable spaces is metrizable in the product topology. For metric spaces, **compactness is equivalent to sequential compactness**. Therefore, it suffices to prove that the product space is sequentially compact.

    Let $\{x^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}$ be an arbitrary sequence in the product space $\prod_{i=1}^{\infty} A_i$.
    We will use the **Cantor diagonal argument** to extract a convergent subsequence.

    Consider the sequence of first coordinates $\{x_1^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}$.
    Since $A_1$ is finite and the sequence has infinitely many terms, by the pigeonhole principle there is some element of $A_1$ that is taken infinitely many times.
    Hence we can extract a subsequence $\{x^{(n, 1)}\}$ from the original sequence such that its first coordinate is constant.

    Next, consider the second coordinates of the subsequence $\{x^{(n, 1)}\}$.
    Since $A_2$ is finite, we can extract from $\{x^{(n, 1)}\}$ a further subsequence $\{x^{(n, 2)}\}$ whose second coordinate is also constant (now both the first and second coordinates are fixed).

    Continuing inductively, suppose we have extracted the $k$-th subsequence $\{x^{(n, k)}\}$. Since $A_{k+1}$ is finite, we can always extract the $(k+1)$-th subsequence $\{x^{(n, k+1)}\}$ whose first $k+1$ coordinates are all constant.

    Now construct the diagonal sequence:

    \[
    y^{(m)} = x^{(m, m)}
    \]

    Consider this diagonal sequence $y^{(m)}$. For any fixed coordinate position $k$, when $m \ge k$, $y^{(m)}$ belongs to the $k$-th subsequence (or a later subsequence), so its $k$-th coordinate is a fixed constant.

    In the product topology, convergence of a sequence is equivalent to coordinate-wise convergence.
    Hence for every fixed $k$, the $k$-th coordinate of the diagonal sequence $y^{(m)}$ is eventually constant, so it converges in the $k$-th coordinate.

    Therefore the diagonal sequence $y^{(m)}$ converges in the product topology to some point of the space.
    This shows that every sequence has a convergent subsequence, so the product space is sequentially compact, and therefore **compact**.

## Problem 8

($p$-adic integers) Fix a prime number $p$. For each $i \in \mathbb{N}_{\ge 1}$, we have the quotient ring $\mathbb{Z}/p^i\mathbb{Z}$ induced by the equivalence relation.
Consider the following set:

\[
\mathbb{Z}_p := \left\{ (a_i)_{i \in \mathbb{N}_{\ge 1}} : a_i \in \mathbb{Z}/p^i\mathbb{Z} \text{ and for all } i, \phi_{i+1}(a_{i+1}) = a_i \right\} \subseteq \prod_{i=1}^{\infty} (\mathbb{Z}/p^i\mathbb{Z})
\]

where $\phi_i: \mathbb{Z}/p^i\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/p^{i-1}\mathbb{Z}$ is the natural surjection. Give each $\mathbb{Z}/p^i\mathbb{Z}$ the discrete topology, and give the product space the product topology.

(i) Prove that $\mathbb{Z}_p$ is closed in the product space $\prod_{i=1}^{\infty} (\mathbb{Z}/p^i\mathbb{Z})$, and is compact.
(ii) For each $i \ge 1$, let $f_i: \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/p^i\mathbb{Z}$ be the standard quotient map. Consider the embedding $f: \mathbb{Z} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{Z}_p$, defined by $f(a) = (f_i(a))_{i \ge 1}$. Prove that $f(\mathbb{Z})$ is dense in $\mathbb{Z}_p$.

??? success "Solution"

    **(i) Prove that $\mathbb{Z}_p$ is closed and compact**

    For any fixed $i \ge 1$, define the map $\psi_i: \prod_{k=1}^{\infty} (\mathbb{Z}/p^k\mathbb{Z}) \to \mathbb{Z}/p^i\mathbb{Z}$ by:

    \[
    \psi_i((a_k)_{k=1}^{\infty}) = \phi_{i+1}(a_{i+1}) - a_i
    \]

    This map $\psi_i$ depends only on the $i$-th and $(i+1)$-th coordinates of the product space. Since projection maps are continuous and algebraic operations on finitely many coordinates are continuous in the discrete topology, $\psi_i$ must be continuous.

    Let $E_i$ be the set defined by the $i$-th compatibility condition:

    \[
    E_i = \{ (a_k) \in \prod (\mathbb{Z}/p^k\mathbb{Z}) : \phi_{i+1}(a_{i+1}) = a_i \} = \psi_i^{-1}(\{0\})
    \]

    Since the singleton $\{0\}$ is closed in the discrete topology and $\psi_i$ is continuous, the preimage $E_i$ is **closed** in the product topology.

    The space $\mathbb{Z}_p$ of $p$-adic integers is the intersection of all compatibility sets:

    \[
    \mathbb{Z}_p = \bigcap_{i=1}^{\infty} E_i
    \]

    An arbitrary intersection of closed sets is closed, hence $\mathbb{Z}_p$ is **closed**.

    On the other hand, each coordinate space $\mathbb{Z}/p^i\mathbb{Z}$ is finite and hence compact in the discrete topology.
    By the **Tychonoff theorem**, the product space $\prod_{i=1}^{\infty} (\mathbb{Z}/p^i\mathbb{Z})$ is compact.

    A closed subset of a compact space is compact. Since $\mathbb{Z}_p$ is closed in the compact product space, $\mathbb{Z}_p$ itself is **compact**.

    **(ii) Prove that $f(\mathbb{Z})$ is dense**

    To prove density, it suffices to show that for every nonempty basic open set $U$ in the subspace topology of $\mathbb{Z}_p$, we have $U \cap f(\mathbb{Z}) \ne \emptyset$.

    Under the product topology, a basic open set $U$ in $\mathbb{Z}_p$ is determined by finitely many coordinate restrictions. Because of the compatibility conditions $\phi_{i+1}(a_{i+1}) = a_i$ in $\mathbb{Z}_p$, higher-order coordinates completely determine the lower-order coordinates.

    Hence every nonempty basic open set in $\mathbb{Z}_p$ can be represented by a restriction on one sufficiently large $N$-th coordinate: there exists a positive integer $N$ and a constant $c \in \mathbb{Z}/p^N\mathbb{Z}$ such that:

    \[
    U = \{ (x_i)_{i=1}^{\infty} \in \mathbb{Z}_p : x_N = c \}
    \]

    Since $c$ is an equivalence class in the quotient ring $\mathbb{Z}/p^N\mathbb{Z}$, we can choose a representative $a \in \mathbb{Z}$ such that:

    \[
    a \equiv c \pmod{p^N}
    \]

    Consider the image $f(a) = (f_i(a))_{i \ge 1}$ of the integer $a$ under the map $f$.
    By construction, its $N$-th coordinate is exactly $f_N(a) = c$.

    Therefore $f(a) \in U$. This shows that $U \cap f(\mathbb{Z})$ is nonempty.
    Since every nonempty open set contains a point of $f(\mathbb{Z})$, we have $\overline{f(\mathbb{Z})} = \mathbb{Z}_p$, i.e. $f(\mathbb{Z})$ is dense in $\mathbb{Z}_p$.

## Problem 9

Consider the subspace $Y = \{0\} \cup \{1/n : n \in \mathbb{N}_{\ge 1}\}$ of the real line $\mathbb{R}$.
Is $Y$ homeomorphic to the one-point compactification $X = (\mathbb{N}_{\ge 1} \cup \{\infty\}, \mathcal{T}')$ of the natural numbers $\mathbb{N}_{\ge 1}$ with the discrete topology? Justify your answer.

??? success "Solution"

    **Conclusion:** Yes, they are homeomorphic.

    **Proof:**

    We first construct a bijection $f: X \to Y$ as follows:

    \[
    f(n) = \frac{1}{n} \quad (\text{for } n \in \mathbb{N}_{\ge 1})
    \]

    \[
    f(\infty) = 0
    \]

    This is clearly a bijection.

    We now prove that $f$ is continuous. We need to check that the preimage under $f$ of every open set in $Y$ is open in $X$.

    Let $V$ be an arbitrary open set in $Y$. Consider two cases:

    * **Case 1:** $0 \notin V$.
      Then $V$ consists entirely of isolated points of the form $1/n$.
      Its preimage $f^{-1}(V)$ is a subset of $\mathbb{N}_{\ge 1}$ and does not contain $\infty$.
      Since $\mathbb{N}_{\ge 1}$ is discrete, in its one-point compactification $X$, every subset not containing $\infty$ is open. Hence $f^{-1}(V)$ is open in $X$.

    * **Case 2:** $0 \in V$.
      Since $V$ is open in the subspace $Y$ of $\mathbb{R}$, there exists $\epsilon > 0$ such that $(-\epsilon, \epsilon) \cap Y \subseteq V$.
      This means that for all $n > 1/\epsilon$, the points $1/n$ belong to $V$.
      In other words, $V$ misses only finitely many points of $Y$ of the form $1/n$.
      Consider its preimage $f^{-1}(V)$: it contains the point $\infty$, and its complement in $\mathbb{N}_{\ge 1}$ is finite.
      By the definition of the one-point compactification, a set containing the point at infinity and having finite complement is exactly an open neighbourhood in the compactification space $X$. Hence $f^{-1}(V)$ is again open.

    This proves that $f$ is continuous.

    Finally, we use an important theorem in general topology: **a continuous bijection from a compact space to a Hausdorff space is necessarily a homeomorphism**.

    * The space $X$ is a one-point compactification, which by definition is a **compact space**.
    * The space $Y$ is a subspace of the metric space $\mathbb{R}$, hence a **Hausdorff space**.

    Since $f: X \to Y$ is a continuous bijection satisfying the “compact domain to Hausdorff codomain” condition, $f$ must be a homeomorphism.

Prove that the product topological space $(\mathbb{R}^\omega, \mathcal{T}_p)$ has no one-point compactification.

    **Proof:**

    According to the fundamental theory of topology, a space $X$ has a Hausdorff one-point compactification if and only if **$X$ itself is locally compact, Hausdorff, and non-compact.**
    The product space $\mathbb{R}^\omega$ is clearly Hausdorff (since it is a product of Hausdorff spaces $\mathbb{R}$).
    We will prove that it is **not locally compact**, so it cannot have a one-point compactification.

    We argue by contradiction. Suppose $(\mathbb{R}^\omega, \mathcal{T}_p)$ is locally compact.

    Consider the origin $\mathbf{0} = (0, 0, \dots) \in \mathbb{R}^\omega$. By local compactness, the origin must have a compact neighbourhood $K$.

    By definition of the product topology basis, since $K$ is a neighbourhood of $\mathbf{0}$, there exists a basic open set $U$ such that:

    \[
    \mathbf{0} \in U \subseteq K
    \]

    In the product topology, a basic open set $U$ can restrict only finitely many coordinates. That is, there exists a positive integer $N$ such that:

    \[
    U = U_1 \times U_2 \times \dots \times U_N \times \mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R} \times \dots
    \]

    where the first $N$ sets $U_i$ are open intervals in $\mathbb{R}$, and from the $(N+1)$-th coordinate onward, all factors must be the whole space $\mathbb{R}$.

    Now consider the projection onto the $(N+1)$-th coordinate $\pi_{N+1}: \mathbb{R}^\omega \to \mathbb{R}$.
    In the product topology the projection map is always continuous.

    Since $K$ is compact and continuous maps send compact sets to compact sets, the projection $\pi_{N+1}(K)$ must be a **compact subset** of $\mathbb{R}$.

    However, from the inclusion $U \subseteq K$, we obtain the corresponding inclusion of projections:

    \[
    \pi_{N+1}(U) \subseteq \pi_{N+1}(K)
    \]

    By the structure of the basic open set $U$, the projection onto the $(N+1)$-th coordinate is the whole real line:

    \[
    \pi_{N+1}(U) = \mathbb{R}
    \]

    Combining steps 8 and 9, we get $\mathbb{R} \subseteq \pi_{N+1}(K)$. Thus the compact set $\pi_{N+1}(K)$ must equal the whole space $\mathbb{R}$.

    But we know that the real line $\mathbb{R}$ is unbounded, and therefore not compact! This is an irreconcilable contradiction.

    The contradiction shows that our assumption was false, i.e. $\mathbb{R}^\omega$ is not locally compact. Lacking local compactness, it cannot admit a one-point compactification.

## Problem 11

Prove that if $X_i$ is separable for every $i \in \mathbb{N}$, then the product space $\left(\prod_{i \in \mathbb{N}} X_i, \mathcal{T}_p\right)$ is also separable.

??? success "Solution"

    **Proof:**

    Since each $X_i$ is separable, for each $i \in \mathbb{N}$ there exists a countable dense subset $A_i \subseteq X_i$.

    In the product space $\prod X_i$, fix a “base point” $x^* = (x_1^*, x_2^*, \dots)$.

    For each $n \in \mathbb{N}$, define the set $D_n$ to be the set of sequences whose first $n$ coordinates belong to $A_i$ and whose remaining coordinates coincide with the base point:

    \[
    D_n = \{ (x_i) \in \prod X_i \mid x_i \in A_i \text{ for } i \le n, \text{ and } x_i = x_i^* \text{ for } i > n \}
    \]

    Clearly each $D_n$ is in bijection with the finite product $A_1 \times A_2 \times \dots \times A_n$. Since a finite product of countable sets is countable, each $D_n$ is countable.

    Let $D = \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty D_n$. As a countable union of countable sets, $D$ must be **countable**.

    We now prove that $D$ is dense in the product topology. Take an arbitrary nonempty basic open set $U = \prod_{i=1}^\infty U_i$ in the product space.

    By definition of the product topology, only finitely many coordinates have $U_i$ not equal to the whole space. That is, there exists a positive integer $N$ such that for all $i > N$, $U_i = X_i$.

    For $i \le N$, since $U_i$ is a nonempty open set in $X_i$ and $A_i$ is dense in $X_i$, there exists a point $y_i \in U_i \cap A_i$.

    Construct a sequence $y = (y_1, y_2, \dots) \in \prod X_i$, where the first $N$ coordinates are the chosen points $y_i$, and all coordinates after $N$ are the base point coordinates $x_i^*$.

    By definition, since only the first $N$ coordinates of $y$ may differ from $x^*$ and these $N$ coordinates all belong to $A_i$, we have $y \in D_N \subseteq D$.

    Also, for $i \le N$, $y_i \in U_i$; for $i > N$, $y_i = x_i^* \in X_i = U_i$. Hence $y \in U$.

    This shows $U \cap D \ne \emptyset$. Since every basic open set contains a point of $D$, $D$ is dense. Having found a countable dense subset, the product space is **separable**.

## Problem 12

Let $(X, d)$ be a metric space. Prove that the following three properties are equivalent:

(i) $X$ is second countable;
(ii) $X$ is a Lindelöf space;
(iii) $X$ is separable.

(Hint: For (ii) $\Rightarrow$ (iii), consider the open cover $\{B(x, 1/n) : x \in X, n \in \mathbb{N}\}$, fix $n$ and first use the Lindelöf property.)

??? success "Solution"

    **Proof:**

    **Proof of (i) $\Rightarrow$ (ii): Second countable $\Rightarrow$ Lindelöf**

    Let $\mathcal{B}$ be a countable basis for $X$ and let $\mathcal{U}$ be an arbitrary open cover of $X$.

    For any point $x \in X$, since $\mathcal{U}$ covers $X$, there exists an open set $U_x \in \mathcal{U}$ such that $x \in U_x$.

    Since $\mathcal{B}$ is a basis, there exists a basis element $B_x \in \mathcal{B}$ such that $x \in B_x \subseteq U_x$.

    Consider the collection $\mathcal{B}' = \{B_x \mid x \in X\}$ of all chosen basis elements. Since $\mathcal{B}$ is countable, the subset $\mathcal{B}'$ must also be countable.

    For each basis element in $\mathcal{B}'$, choose one open set in $\mathcal{U}$ that contains it. The selected family is a subcollection of $\mathcal{U}$ and is at most countable.

    This countable family still covers all of $X$. Therefore every open cover has a countable subcover, so $X$ is Lindelöf.

    **Proof of (ii) $\Rightarrow$ (iii): Lindelöf $\Rightarrow$ separable**

    For any fixed positive integer $n$, consider the family of open balls of radius $1/n$ centered at every point of $X$:

    \[
    \mathcal{U}_n = \left\{ B\left(x, \frac{1}{n}\right) : x \in X \right\}
    \]

    Clearly $\mathcal{U}_n$ is an open cover of $X$. Since $X$ is Lindelöf, there exists a countable subcover.

    That is, there exist countably many points $x_1^{(n)}, x_2^{(n)}, \dots \in X$ such that the corresponding open balls cover $X$:

    \[
    X \subseteq \bigcup_{i=1}^\infty B\left(x_i^{(n)}, \frac{1}{n}\right)
    \]

    Let $D_n = \{x_i^{(n)} \mid i \in \mathbb{N}\}$ be the set of these countably many centers. $D_n$ is countable.

    Let $D = \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty D_n$. A countable union of countable sets is still **countable**.

    We prove that $D$ is dense in $X$. Take any $y \in X$ and any $\epsilon > 0$. There exists a positive integer $n$ such that $1/n < \epsilon$.

    Since the balls with centers in $D_n$ cover $X$, there exists some $x_k^{(n)} \in D_n \subseteq D$ such that $y \in B(x_k^{(n)}, 1/n)$.

    This means $d(y, x_k^{(n)}) < 1/n < \epsilon$, so $x_k^{(n)} \in B(y, \epsilon)$. Thus $D$ intersects every open neighbourhood of $y$. Hence $D$ is dense in $X$, and $X$ is separable.

    **Proof of (iii) $\Rightarrow$ (i): Separable $\Rightarrow$ second countable**

    Suppose $X$ is separable, so there exists a countable dense subset $D$.

    Construct the family of open balls:

    \[
    \mathcal{B} = \left\{ B\left(x, \frac{1}{n}\right) : x \in D, n \in \mathbb{N} \right\}
    \]

    Since $D$ and $\mathbb{N}$ are countable, $\mathcal{B}$ is a **countable family**. We claim that $\mathcal{B}$ is a basis for $X$.

    Take an arbitrary open set $U$ in $X$ and a point $y \in U$. Since $U$ is open, there exists $\epsilon > 0$ such that $B(y, \epsilon) \subseteq U$.

    Choose an integer $n$ large enough so that $1/n < \epsilon / 2$.

    Since $D$ is dense in $X$, there exists a point $x \in D$ inside the open ball $B(y, 1/n)$. That is, $d(y, x) < 1/n$.

    Now the basis element $B(x, 1/n) \in \mathcal{B}$ contains $y$, because $d(y, x) < 1/n$.

    For any $z \in B(x, 1/n)$, by the triangle inequality:

    \[
    d(z, y) \le d(z, x) + d(x, y) < \frac{1}{n} + \frac{1}{n} = \frac{2}{n} < \epsilon
    \]

    Hence $B(x, 1/n) \subseteq B(y, \epsilon) \subseteq U$. Since every open set can be approximated by elements of $\mathcal{B}$, $\mathcal{B}$ is indeed a countable basis for the space. Hence $X$ is second countable.

## Problem 13

Let $(X, d)$ be a pseudometric space. Let $\mathcal{T}_d$ be the topology on $X$ generated by the open balls

\[
\left\{ B(x, \epsilon) : x \in X, \epsilon \in \mathbb{R}_{>0} \right\}
\]

where $B(x, \epsilon) := \{y \in X : d(x, y) < \epsilon\}$. Prove that the topological space $(X, \mathcal{T}_d)$ is a $T_0$ space if and only if $d$ is a metric.

??? success "Solution"

    **Proof of necessity ($\Rightarrow$):**

    Suppose $(X, \mathcal{T}_d)$ is a $T_0$ space. To prove that $d$ is a metric, it suffices to prove that for all $x \ne y$, $d(x, y) > 0$ (since a pseudometric already satisfies symmetry, triangle inequality, and nonnegativity).

    For $x \ne y$, since the space is $T_0$, there exists an open set $U$ containing one of the points but not the other. Suppose, without loss of generality, that $x \in U$ and $y \notin U$.

    Since $\mathcal{T}_d$ is generated by open balls (in fact, the open balls themselves form a basis for the pseudometric topology), there exists an open ball $B(p, r)$ such that:

    \[
    x \in B(p, r) \subseteq U
    \]

    Since $y \notin U$, we must have $y \notin B(p, r)$.

    By the definition of open balls, $d(p, x) < r$ and $d(p, y) \ge r$.

    Using the triangle inequality for pseudometrics, $d(p, y) \le d(p, x) + d(x, y)$, hence:

    \[
    d(x, y) \ge d(p, y) - d(p, x) > r - r = 0
    \]

    Since $d(x, y) > 0$ for all $x \ne y$, $d$ satisfies the positivity condition and is therefore a genuine metric.

    **Proof of sufficiency ($\Leftarrow$):**

    Suppose $d$ is a metric. Take any two distinct points $x \ne y$ in $X$.

    Since the metric satisfies positivity, we have $d(x, y) = r > 0$.

    Choose $\epsilon = r / 2 > 0$ and consider the open ball $B(x, \epsilon)$.

    Clearly $x \in B(x, \epsilon)$.

    If $y \in B(x, \epsilon)$, then by definition $d(x, y) < \epsilon = r / 2$, contradicting $d(x, y) = r$.

    Hence $y \notin B(x, \epsilon)$.

    We have found an open set $B(x, \epsilon)$ that contains $x$ but not $y$. Thus the space satisfies the $T_0$ separation axiom.

## Problem 14

For a topological space $(X, \mathcal{T})$, prove the following statements:

(i) If $X$ is a $T_3$ space, then for any distinct $x, y \in X$, there exist open sets $U, V$ such that $x \in U$, $y \in V$, and $\overline{U} \cap \overline{V} = \emptyset$.
(ii) If $X$ is a $T_4$ space, then for any disjoint closed sets $A, B \subseteq X$, there exist open sets $U, V$ such that $A \subseteq U$, $B \subseteq V$, and $\overline{U} \cap \overline{V} = \emptyset$.

??? success "Solution"

    **(i) Proof for $T_3$ spaces**

    Suppose $X$ is a $T_3$ space, i.e. it is regular and satisfies the $T_1$ separation axiom. For any distinct $x, y \in X$, by the $T_1$ property the singleton $\{y\}$ is closed.

    Since $x \notin \{y\}$ and $\{y\}$ is closed, by regularity there exist disjoint open sets $U_1$ and $W_1$ such that:

    \[
    x \in U_1 \quad \text{and} \quad y \in W_1
    \]

    Since $U_1 \cap W_1 = \emptyset$, we have $U_1 \subseteq X \setminus W_1$. Because $W_1$ is open, $X \setminus W_1$ is closed.

    Since $U_1$ is contained in the closed set $X \setminus W_1$, its closure must also be contained in it:

    \[
    \overline{U_1} \subseteq X \setminus W_1
    \]

    This means $\overline{U_1} \cap W_1 = \emptyset$, hence $y \notin \overline{U_1}$.

    Now we have the point $y$ and the closed set $\overline{U_1}$ not containing $y$. Applying regularity again, there exist disjoint open sets $V$ and $W_2$ such that:

    \[
    y \in V \quad \text{and} \quad \overline{U_1} \subseteq W_2
    \]

    Similarly, since $V \cap W_2 = \emptyset$, we deduce $\overline{V} \subseteq X \setminus W_2$.

    Combining $\overline{U_1} \subseteq W_2$, we obtain $\overline{U_1} \subseteq X \setminus \overline{V}$, i.e.:

    \[
    \overline{U_1} \cap \overline{V} = \emptyset
    \]

    Let $U = U_1$. We have found open sets $U$ and $V$ such that $x \in U$, $y \in V$, and $\overline{U} \cap \overline{V} = \emptyset$.

    **(ii) Proof for $T_4$ spaces**

    Suppose $X$ is a $T_4$ space, i.e. it is normal and satisfies the $T_1$ separation axiom. Let $A, B$ be two disjoint closed sets.

    By normality, there exist disjoint open sets $U_1$ and $W_1$ such that:

    \[
    A \subseteq U_1 \quad \text{and} \quad B \subseteq W_1
    \]

    As before, since $U_1 \cap W_1 = \emptyset$, we have $U_1 \subseteq X \setminus W_1$, and taking closures:

    \[
    \overline{U_1} \subseteq X \setminus W_1
    \]

    Since $B \subseteq W_1$, we get $\overline{U_1} \cap B = \emptyset$.

    Now we have two new disjoint closed sets: $\overline{U_1}$ and $B$. Applying normality again, there exist disjoint open sets $U_2$ and $V$ such that:

    \[
    \overline{U_1} \subseteq U_2 \quad \text{and} \quad B \subseteq V
    \]

    Since $U_2 \cap V = \emptyset$, similarly we deduce $\overline{V} \subseteq X \setminus U_2$.

    Because $\overline{U_1} \subseteq U_2$, this immediately implies:

    \[
    \overline{U_1} \cap \overline{V} = \emptyset
    \]

    Let $U = U_1$. We clearly have $A \subseteq U$ (by step 2) and $B \subseteq V$ (by step 5), and the closures are disjoint: $\overline{U} \cap \overline{V} = \emptyset$. This completes the proof.

## Problem 15

Prove that every locally compact Hausdorff space is regular, i.e. “locally compact + $T_2 \Rightarrow T_3$”.

??? success "Solution"

    **Proof:**

    Let $X$ be a locally compact Hausdorff space. Since $X$ is Hausdorff (i.e. $T_2$), it satisfies the $T_1$ separation axiom. To prove regularity, it suffices to show that for every closed set $A \subseteq X$ and every point $x \notin A$, there exist disjoint open sets separating them.

    Since $X$ is locally compact, the point $x$ has a **compact neighbourhood** $K$ containing $x$.

    By the definition of neighbourhood, there exists an open set $W$ such that:

    \[
    x \in W \subseteq K
    \]

    Since compact subsets of Hausdorff spaces are closed, $K$ is closed.

    Define $C = A \cap K$. Since both $A$ and $K$ are closed, $C$ is a closed subset of $K$. A closed subset of a compact set is compact, so $C$ is **compact**.

    Since $x \notin A$, clearly $x \notin C$. Now we need to separate the point $x$ from the compact set $C$ in the Hausdorff space $X$.

    For each $y \in C$, since $x \ne y$ and $X$ is Hausdorff, there exist disjoint open sets $U_y$ and $V_y$ such that:

    \[
    x \in U_y \quad \text{and} \quad y \in V_y
    \]

    The family $\{V_y \mid y \in C\}$ is an open cover of the compact set $C$. By compactness, there exist finitely many points $y_1, y_2, \dots, y_n \in C$ such that:

    \[
    C \subseteq \bigcup_{i=1}^n V_{y_i}
    \]

    Now define:

    \[
    V = \bigcup_{i=1}^n V_{y_i} \quad \text{and} \quad U = \bigcap_{i=1}^n U_{y_i}
    \]

    Clearly $U$ and $V$ are disjoint open sets and satisfy $x \in U$ and $C \subseteq V$.

    Now use $U, V$ to construct two new open sets $O_1$ and $O_2$ separating $x$ from the original closed set $A$:

    \[
    O_1 = U \cap W
    \]

    \[
    O_2 = V \cup (X \setminus K)
    \]

    **Check that $O_1, O_2$ are open and contain the desired sets:**
  
    * Since $U$ and $W$ are open, $O_1$ is open. Also $x \in U$ and $x \in W$, so $x \in O_1$.

    * Since $V$ is open and $K$ is closed (so $X \setminus K$ is open), $O_2$ is open.

    * For any $a \in A$: if $a \in K$, then $a \in A \cap K = C$, hence $a \in V \subseteq O_2$; if $a \notin K$, then $a \in X \setminus K \subseteq O_2$. In either case $A \subseteq O_2$.

    **Check that $O_1$ and $O_2$ are disjoint:**

    \[
    O_1 \cap O_2 = (U \cap W) \cap (V \cup (X \setminus K)) = (U \cap W \cap V) \cup (U \cap W \cap (X \setminus K))
    \]

    * The first part is empty because $U \cap V = \emptyset$, so $U \cap W \cap V = \emptyset$.

    * The second part is empty because $W \subseteq K$, so $W \cap (X \setminus K) = \emptyset$.
  
    * Hence $O_1 \cap O_2 = \emptyset$.

    In summary, we have found two disjoint open sets $O_1$ and $O_2$ containing $x$ and the closed set $A$ respectively. Therefore the space is **regular** ($T_3$).
