# Chapter 12: Countability and Separation Axioms

In point‑set topology, countability axioms and separation axioms are two core tools for describing the degree of fineness of the structure of a topological space. Countability axioms (such as first countability, second countability, separability, and the Lindelöf property) restrict the cardinality of a “basis” or a “dense subset” of the topological space; separation axioms (such as $T_0$, $T_1$, etc.) describe to what extent points and points, or points and closed sets, can be separated by open sets. This chapter will explore these axioms in detail and their relationships.

---

## 1. First Countable and Second Countable Spaces

When studying the local properties of a topological space, we often hope that each point has a countable neighbourhood system nearby – this is the concept of first countability. If the entire space possesses a countable topological basis, it is called second countable.

!!! info "Definition 12.1 (First Countable Space)"

    A topological space $(X, \mathcal{T})$ is called **first countable** if for every point $x \in X$ there exists a **countable open neighbourhood basis**

    \[
    \mathcal{B}_x := \{B_{x,i}\}_{i \in \mathbb{N}}
    \]

    satisfying: for any open set $U$ containing $x$, there exists some $B_{x,i} \in \mathcal{B}_x$ such that

    \[
    x \in B_{x,i} \subseteq U.
    \]

!!! info "Definition 12.2 (Second Countable Space)"

    A topological space $(X, \mathcal{T})$ is called **second countable** if it possesses a **countable basis**

    \[
    \mathcal{B} := \{B_i\}_{i \in \mathbb{N}}
    \]

    such that every open set in $\mathcal{T}$ can be expressed as a union of elements of $\mathcal{B}$ .

**Basic relation:**
Clearly, **second countability implies first countability**. Indeed, if the space has a global countable basis $\mathcal{B}$, then for any point $x$, we simply select all basis elements that contain $x$, i.e., $\mathcal{B}_x = \{B_i \in \mathcal{B} : x \in B_i\}$, which automatically forms a countable local open neighbourhood basis at $x$.

**Example: Countable product space**

Consider the countably infinite‑dimensional real space $\mathbb{R}^\omega = \mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}$ with the product topology $\mathcal{T}_p$. A basis element has the form

\[
\prod_{i \in \mathbb{N}} (a_i, b_i),
\]

where, except for finitely many $i$, almost every interval is the whole $\mathbb{R}$ (i.e., for almost all $i$, $(a_i, b_i) = \mathbb{R}$).

---

### 1.1 Hereditary Properties of Countability Axioms

Countability axioms behave well under taking subspaces and countable products.

!!! success "Lemma 12.1 (Hereditary for subspaces)"

    If $(X, \mathcal{T})$ is first countable (or second countable), then any subspace $A \subseteq X$ with the subspace topology is also first countable (or second countable).

!!! success "Lemma 12.2 (Hereditary for countable products)"

    If for every $i \in \mathbb{N}$ the space $X_i$ is first countable (or second countable), then the countable product space $(\prod_{i \in \mathbb{N}} X_i, \mathcal{T}_p)$ is also first countable (or second countable).

??? proof "Proof of Lemma 12.2 (for first countability) (Click to expand)"

    Assume each $X_i$ is first countable. Take an arbitrary point in the product space:

    \[
    x = (x_1, x_2, \dots, x_i, \dots) \in \prod_{i \in \mathbb{N}} X_i.
    \]

    Since each $X_i$ is first countable, at each coordinate $x_i$ there exists a countable open neighbourhood basis $\mathcal{B}_{x_i}$.

    Define a local basis at $x$ in the product space by

    \[
    \mathcal{B}_x := \left\{ \prod_{i \in \mathbb{N}} B_i : B_i \in \mathcal{B}_{x_i},\text{ and for all but finitely many } i, B_i = X_i \right\}.
    \]

    Because each local basis $\mathcal{B}_{x_i}$ is countable, and when forming the product we only allow finitely many coordinates to take basis elements other than the whole space, the set of such finite combinations is still countable.

    Hence $\mathcal{B}_x$ is a countable set. One can easily verify that $\mathcal{B}_x$ indeed forms an open neighbourhood basis at $x$ for the product topology. This proves that the product space is first countable. $\square$

## 2. Separable Spaces and Lindelöf Spaces

We now examine two other global topological properties related to countability: separability (restricting the cardinality of a dense set) and the Lindelöf property (restricting the size of open covers).

!!! info "Definition 12.3 (Separable Space)"

    A topological space $(X, \mathcal{T})$ is called **separable** if it possesses a **countable dense subset**.

    That is, there exists a countable set $S = \{x_i\}_{i \in \mathbb{N}} \subseteq X$ such that its closure equals the whole space:

    \[
    \bar{S} = X.
    \]

!!! info "Definition 12.4 (Lindelöf Space)"

    A topological space $(X, \mathcal{T})$ is called a **Lindelöf space** if every open cover $\mathcal{A} = \{A_i\}_{i \in I}$ of $X$ has a **countable subcover**.

---

### 2.1 Powerful Consequences of Second Countability

Second countability is the strongest countability axiom; it directly implies separability and the Lindelöf property.

!!! success "Proposition 12.3"

    If a topological space $(X, \mathcal{T})$ is **second countable**, then:

    * (1) It is **Lindelöf**.

    * (2) It is **separable**.

??? proof "Proof of Proposition 12.3 (Click to expand)"

    Let $\mathcal{B} = \{B_n\}_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ be a countable topological basis of $X$.

    **Proof of (1): Second countable $\Rightarrow$ Lindelöf**

    Let $\mathcal{A} = \{A_i\}_{i \in I}$ be an arbitrary open cover of $X$. For each $x \in X$, because $\mathcal{A}$ covers $X$, there exists some $A_i \in \mathcal{A}$ with $x \in A_i$.

    Since $\mathcal{B}$ is a basis, there exists some basis element $B_{n_x} \in \mathcal{B}$ such that

    \[
    x \in B_{n_x} \subseteq A_i.
    \]

    Letting $x$ range over $X$, the selected basis elements form a subfamily $\mathcal{B}'$ of $\mathcal{B}$. Because $\mathcal{B}$ itself is countable, $\mathcal{B}'$ is countable (or finite).

    For each basis element $B_k \in \mathcal{B}'$, choose an arbitrary open set $A_{i_k}$ from the original cover $\mathcal{A}$ that contains it (i.e., $B_k \subseteq A_{i_k}$).

    Set $\mathcal{A}' = \{A_{i_k}\}$. Since $\mathcal{B}'$ is countable, $\mathcal{A}'$ is also countable. Because $\mathcal{B}'$ covers $X$ and each $B_k \subseteq A_{i_k}$, $\mathcal{A}'$ also covers $X$. Thus we have found a countable subcover, proving the Lindelöf property.

    **Proof of (2): Second countable $\Rightarrow$ Separable**

    From the countable basis $\mathcal{B} = \{B_n\}_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$, for each non‑empty basis element $B_n \ne \emptyset$, pick an arbitrary point $x_n \in B_n$.

    Let $S = \{x_n\}_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$. Clearly $S$ is a countable subset. We claim that $\bar{S} = X$.

    Using an equivalent characterisation of density: we need to show that for every non‑empty open set $U \subseteq X$, $U \cap S \ne \emptyset$.

    Because $\mathcal{B}$ is a basis, every non‑empty open set $U$ contains some basis element $B_k \in \mathcal{B}$.
    By the construction of $S$, the point $x_k$ belongs to $B_k$, hence also to $U$.

    Moreover $x_k \in S$, so $x_k \in U \cap S$; therefore $U \cap S \ne \emptyset$. This proves that $S$ is dense in $X$, so $X$ is separable. $\square$

## 3. Relationships between Topological Properties and Classical Counterexamples

In metric spaces, many countability and compactness concepts are equivalent, but in general topological spaces their relationships form a one‑way implication structure.

**Diagram of topological properties:**

* **In general topological spaces:**
  Second countable $\implies$ First countable
  Second countable $\implies$ Separable
  Second countable $\implies$ Lindelöf

* **In metric spaces, the following are equivalent:**
  Second countable $\iff$ Separable $\iff$ Lindelöf

*(Note: In metric spaces, sequential compactness is also equivalent to compactness, and further connections exist under the condition of being closed and bounded.)*

---

### 3.1 Classical Counterexample: The Lower Limit Topology $\mathbb{R}_l$

The **lower limit topology** $\mathbb{R}_l$ on the real line is an extremely classical counterexample used to distinguish these topological properties. Its basis consists of half‑open intervals:

\[
\mathcal{B} := \{ [a, b) : a < b, a, b \in \mathbb{R} \}.
\]

!!! example "Properties of the Lower Limit Topology $\mathbb{R}_l$"

    * **(1) It is first countable.**

    For any $x \in \mathbb{R}$, we can construct a countable local neighbourhood basis:

    \[
    \mathcal{B}_x := \left\{ \left[ x, x + \frac{1}{n} \right) : n \in \mathbb{N}_{\ge 1} \right\}.
    \]

    It is easy to verify that any open set containing $x$ must contain some element of $\mathcal{B}_x$.

    * **(2) It is NOT second countable.**

    Suppose a basis $\mathcal{B}'$ existed. For each real number $x \in \mathbb{R}$, consider the open set $[x, x+1)$. Since $\mathcal{B}'$ is a basis, there must exist some $B_x \in \mathcal{B}'$ such that

    \[
    x \in B_x \subseteq [x, x+1).
    \]

    This forces the infimum of $B_x$ to be exactly $x$, i.e., $\inf B_x = x$.

    If $x \ne y$, then necessarily $B_x \ne B_y$ (because their minima/infima are different). Thus we would need distinct basis elements for uncountably many distinct real numbers $x$. Hence the cardinality of $\mathcal{B}'$ would be at least $\aleph_1$, i.e., the basis must be uncountable!

    * **(3) It is separable.**

    The set of rational numbers $\mathbb{Q}$ is still dense in the lower limit topology, i.e., $\bar{\mathbb{Q}} = \mathbb{R}$ (with respect to $\mathcal{T}_l$). Moreover $\mathbb{Q}$ is countable.

    * **(4) It is Lindelöf.**

    (The proof is omitted here, but $\mathbb{R}_l$ is indeed Lindelöf.)

**Conclusion:** $\mathbb{R}_l$ is separable and Lindelöf, but it is not second countable. This breaks the equivalence of the three properties in metric spaces and also shows that $\mathbb{R}_l$ cannot be metrizable.

## 4. Separation Axioms

Separation axioms investigate to what extent “point from point” and “point from closed set” can be separated by open sets in a topological space. From weakest to strongest, the main ones are:

\[
T_0 \implies T_1 \implies T_2 \text{ (Hausdorff)} \implies T_3 \text{ (Regular)} \implies T_4 \text{ (Normal)}.
\]

!!! info "Definition 12.5 ($T_0$ Space)"

    A topological space $(X, \mathcal{T})$ is called a **$T_0$ space (Kolmogorov space)** if for any two distinct points $x \ne y$, **at least one** open set exists that contains one point but not the other.

    That is, either there exists an open set $U$ with $x \in U$ and $y \notin U$, or there exists an open set $V$ with $y \in V$ and $x \notin V$.

!!! info "Definition 12.6 ($T_1$ Space)"

    A topological space $(X, \mathcal{T})$ is called a **$T_1$ space (Fréchet space)** if for any two distinct points $x \ne y$, **each point has an open set that contains it but not the other**.

    That is, there exists an open set $U$ with $x \in U$ and $y \notin U$, **and** there exists an open set $V$ with $y \in V$ and $x \notin V$.

**Difference between $T_0$ and $T_1$ and a counterexample:**
The $T_1$ condition is strictly stronger than $T_0$. There exist spaces that are $T_0$ but not $T_1$.
A classic counterexample is the **Sierpiński space**: let $X = \{a, b\}$ with topology $\mathcal{T} = \{\emptyset, \{a\}, \{a, b\}\}$.
For $a \ne b$, the open set $\{a\}$ contains $a$ but not $b$ (so $T_0$ holds).
However, there is no open set containing $b$ but not $a$ (so $T_1$ fails).
